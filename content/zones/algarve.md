# Zona: Algarve

## Identity

The Algarve is Portugal's southern strip: 4,997 km², sixteen concelhos, sixty-seven freguesias and 467,343 residents at the 2021 census. The name is Arabic — *al-Gharb*, "the west" — and the region was the last part of the country taken from the Moors, by D. Afonso III in 1249. That eight-century Moorish layer is not decoration here; it is in the place names (Albufeira, Almancil, Alfarroba, Alte), in the flat *açoteia* roof terraces of Olhão and Fuseta, in the cataplana, and in the almond, fig and carob trees.

Residents divide the region before anything else into **barlavento** and **sotavento** — windward and leeward, west of Faro and east of Faro. Barlavento is Albufeira, Aljezur, Lagoa, Lagos, Monchique, Portimão, Silves, Vila do Bispo: limestone cliffs, coves, surf. Sotavento is Alcoutim, Castro Marim, Faro, Loulé, Olhão, São Brás de Alportel, Tavira, Vila Real de Santo António: the Ria Formosa, sandbar islands, salt pans, warmer water. The second division is north–south, in three bands: **serra** (the hills), **barrocal** (the limestone middle, orchards and smallholdings) and **litoral** (the coast, where nearly everyone actually lives).

The population roughly triples in high season. All the figures in this file are **2021 census** unless marked otherwise. Loulé is the largest concelho (72,348), then Faro (67,650) and Portimão (59,845). Albufeira is the most multicultural municipality in Portugal — about 22.5% of its permanent residents were born abroad. The British are the largest foreign resident group, Brazilians the second; there are also substantial Ukrainian, Romanian, Moldovan, Indian, Nepalese and Bangladeshi communities working in hotelaria, construção, agriculture and the *pescas*. Regional GDP was €14.3 billion in 2024, with GDP per capita of €29,302 — second-highest in the country.

The thing to understand about Algarve identity is the double life. There is the Algarve of arrivals halls and August, and there is the Algarve of a wet Tuesday in February when half the restaurants on the seafront have a handwritten sign saying *encerrado — reabre em Março*. Residents live in the second one, and treat the first as weather.

## Geography & climate

Mediterranean climate, Köppen Csa/Csb. Sunshine runs from about 2,600 hours a year up in the Serra de Monchique to well over 3,100 hours on the south coast — among the sunniest figures in Europe. Summer averages 28–30 °C on the coast and 32–35 °C inland; winter averages 16–17 °C. Rainfall is 450–1,200 mm depending where you stand, essentially all of it between October and February, and often torrential — a year's water in three afternoons, roads flooded, then nothing.

**Serra de Monchique** is the northern wall, volcanic-syenite, greener and wetter than the rest, topped by **Fóia** at 902 m — the highest point in the Algarve. **Picota** is its lower neighbour. This is cork oak, chestnut, eucalyptus and *medronheiro* country, and it burns: the 2018 fire was the defining event of recent local memory.

**Ria Formosa** is the region's great feature: a tidal lagoon running roughly 60 km along the sotavento coast, 18,400 hectares, protected as a Reserva Natural from 1978 and as a **Parque Natural** by Decreto-Lei 373/87 of 9 December 1987, and a Ramsar site. It is sheltered from the Atlantic by two peninsulas — **Ancão** and **Cacela** — and five barrier islands: **Barreta** (Ilha Deserta), **Culatra**, **Armona**, **Tavira** and **Cabanas**. It fronts five concelhos: Loulé, Faro, Olhão, Tavira and Vila Real de Santo António. It is a working landscape: *viveiros* for oysters and clams, *salinas*, *sapal* salt marsh, and channels (*esteiros*) that only open at certain tides.

At the eastern end the **Rio Guadiana** is the Spanish border, with the **Reserva Natural do Sapal de Castro Marim e Vila Real de Santo António** on its bank. At the western end, **Cabo de São Vicente** and **Sagres** sit inside the Parque Natural do Sudoeste Alentejano e Costa Vicentina — the southwesternmost point of mainland Europe, and reliably the windiest place in the zone.

Two roads define the region east–west. The **EN125** runs 156 km from Vila do Bispo to Vila Real de Santo António, still through the middle of Olhão, Lagoa and Lagos, with bypasses around Tavira, Faro and Portimão; it has a long-standing reputation for accidents. The **A22 / Via do Infante** runs 133 km from **Bensafrim**, at the Lagos end, to **Vila Real de Santo António** and the Guadiana bridge into Spain.

## Landmarks & buildings

- **Cidade Velha, Faro** — the walled old town, entered through the **Arco da Vila**, with storks nesting on top of it. Inside: the Largo da Sé, the Museu Municipal in the old convent, and cobbled lanes that go quiet at seven in the evening.
- **Teatro Lethes**, Faro — the small Italian-style 19th-century theatre; and the modern **Teatro Municipal de Faro (Teatro das Figuras)** out towards the airport.
- **Universidade do Algarve**, Faro — around ten thousand students across the Penha and Gambelas campuses. The single biggest reason young Algarvios do not have to leave.
- **Aeroporto de Faro**, opened 1965, past ten million passengers a year. For residents it is not glamour, it is the road to and from family, and the reason the EN125 behaves the way it does in July.
- **Palácio de Estói** — rococo-romantic palace above the village of Estói, about 10 km from Faro, with three terraced gardens, azulejo panels, busts and the *Fonte de Ossónoba*. Classified since 1977, restored, and a pousada since Easter 2009. The Roman ruins of **Milreu** are next door.
- **Castelo de Silves** — the great red sandstone fortress of the Moorish capital of the *al-Gharb*, known then as **Xelb**. Below it the **Sé Catedral de Silves**, the **Ponte Antiga** over the Arade, and the **Cruz de Portugal**.
- **Ponte Antiga de Tavira** over the Rio Gilão, with the castle and Santa Maria do Castelo above it.
- **Mercados de Olhão** — the two brick pavilions on the Avenida 5 de Outubro, with their glazed corner towers, inaugurated in 1915–16. The single most recognisable building in the eastern Algarve.
- **Museu de Portimão**, in the former **Feu Hermanos** sardine cannery on the riverside, which began operating in 1902. The best explanation anywhere of what this coast did for a living before tourism.
- **Autódromo Internacional do Algarve**, Portimão — inaugurated 2 November 2008.
- **Mercado de Escravos**, Lagos, on the Praça do Infante — the site described as probably Europe's first slave market, now the Núcleo Museológico. **Ponta da Piedade** and the town walls are the other Lagos set pieces.
- **Fortaleza de Sagres** with its huge *rosa dos ventos* compass rose, and the **Farol do Cabo de São Vicente**. Note for accuracy: there is no documentary evidence that Henry the Navigator's "School of Sagres" ever existed — historians treat it as legend.
- **Vila Real de Santo António** — a Pombaline new town, chartered 30 December 1773, first stone laid 17 March 1774, finished 13 May 1776. The **Praça Marquês de Pombal** with its radiating black-and-white *calçada* is the clearest piece of Enlightenment town planning in the south. Monte Gordo's casino dates from 1934.
- **Caldas de Monchique** — the thermal spa in a wooded hollow, used since Roman times.
- **Castelo de Paderne** (Albufeira) and **Castelo de Loulé** — the inland fortifications residents actually walk their dogs around.

## Churches & religious sites

- **Sé de Faro**, Largo da Sé. Built on a site that held a Roman temple and then a mosque; construction from 1251. Sacked and burned by the Earl of Essex's English force in 1596, then wrecked again by the 1755 earthquake — the massive Romanesque-Gothic bell tower is one of the few things that survived both. Climb it for the Ria Formosa.
- **Igreja do Carmo**, Faro. Founded 1713, rebuilt on a larger plan from 1747 under the master mason Diogo Tavares, west tower finished 1878. Deep gilded *talha dourada* inside. Attached to it is the **Capela dos Ossos** of 1816, walls and vault lined with bones from the Carmelite cemetery, with the inscription over the door: *"Pára aqui a considerar que a este estado hás-de chegar."*
- **Santuário de Nossa Senhora da Piedade**, Loulé — the **Mãe Soberana**. The original hermitage was built in 1553 on the hill above the town; a much larger modern temple stands beside it. The venerated Mannerist wooden image is 90 × 40 cm. This is described as the largest religious gathering south of Fátima.
- **Igreja de Santa Maria do Castelo**, Tavira. Thirteenth century, built on the mosque after D. Paio Peres Correia took the town in 1242. Gothic portal with four archivolts; inside are the tombs of the seven Christian knights and the remains of Paio Peres Correia himself. National Monument since 1910. **Igreja da Misericórdia de Tavira** is the other must-know.
- **Igreja de São Lourenço de Almancil** — a single-nave Baroque church whose entire interior, dome included, is lined with 1730 azulejos by **Policarpo de Oliveira Bernardes**, telling the life of St Lawrence. Classified Imóvel de Interesse Público since 1946.
- **Igreja de Santo António**, Lagos — the soldiers' church, restored in 1769 after the earthquake, famous for its *talha dourada*.
- **Igreja Matriz de Nossa Senhora do Rosário**, Olhão, inaugurated 1715, with the **Capela de Nosso Senhor dos Aflitos** attached — the chapel of the fishing town's own devotion, historically the place families gathered when boats were out in bad weather.
- **Sé Catedral de Silves**, **Igreja Matriz de Monchique** with its twisted Manueline portal, and the ruined **Convento de Nossa Senhora do Desterro** above Monchique.

## Food & drink

**Cataplana** is both the pan and the dish. The pan is two concave metal halves — traditionally copper or brass, now usually aluminium with a copper finish — joined by a hinge and closed with two side clamps, so the food steams sealed over a low heat. It is of Arab origin and it is the Algarve's own object. Classic versions: *amêijoas na cataplana*, *cataplana de marisco*, *cataplana de bacalhau*. It arrives at the table shut, and it is opened in front of you.

**Xarém** is the region's cornmeal porridge — *farinha de milho* stirred into water with *toucinho* or chouriço, banha, coentros — and belongs above all to **Olhão** and the Ria Formosa towns. Eaten with bivalves: **xarém com conquilhas** and **xarém com amêijoas**. It was a finalist in the 7 Maravilhas da Gastronomia Portuguesa.

**Conquilhas** are the small wedge clams of these sands. *Conquilhas à algarvia* — alho, azeite, vinho branco, manteiga, coentros frescos, cooked just until they open — was also a 7 Maravilhas finalist, as were **lulinhas fritas à algarvia**. Other everyday plates: *arroz de marisco*, *lulas recheadas à algarvia*, and **salada algarvia** (tomate, cebola, pimento, orégãos, azeite) which comes to the table whether you ordered it or not.

**Sardinha assada** is a June thing above all — Santos Populares — and a Portimão thing in particular, grilled on the riverside opposite Ferragudo. Salted the night before with *sal grosso*, cooked in a *grelhador duplo* over embers with no flame, and eaten off a thick slice of bread that soaks up the fat, with *pimentos assados* and *batata cozida*. A gathering to do this is a **sardinhada**.

**Frango à Guia / franguinho da Guia** — the small spatchcocked chicken of **Guia**, Albufeira, which calls itself the capital of roast chicken. Marinade: azeite, alho, malagueta, colorau, sumo de limão, aguardente, orégãos, sal grosso. There is a roundabout in Guia on the EN125 built to celebrate the chicken and the local wine, which tells you how seriously it is taken.

**Percebes** (*Pollicipes pollicipes*) come off the rocks of the southwest, the Sagres and Costa Vicentina coast. The *percebeiros* who harvest them work exposed rock in a swell; it is genuinely dangerous work and the price reflects it. Boiled in salt water, nothing else.

**Atum** is the eastern Algarve. **Tavira** and **Vila Real de Santo António** lived on the *almadrava* — the great fixed trap-net tuna fishery — and then on canning. The *armação* vocabulary survives even where the fishery does not.

**Salt.** *Salinas* around **Castro Marim**, **Tavira**, **Olhão** and the Ria Formosa produce traditional sea salt and **flor de sal**, the fine crust skimmed off the surface of the evaporation pans by hand. It is a summer harvest — the warm months, roughly June to August — and the man who reads the pans and decides when to move each stage is the **marnoto**.

**Sweets.** **Dom Rodrigos** are the Algarve's signature convent sweet and are particularly associated with **Lagos**: *fios de ovos*, almond, sugar, cinnamon, twisted up in a screw of coloured foil. The wider family is *doçaria de amêndoa e figo* — morgado, queijinhos de figo, doce fino — built on the two crops the region has always had. **Tarte / torta de alfarroba** uses carob.

**Medronho.** The *medronheiro* (*Arbutus unedo*) grows wild on poor schist soils in the serra. The fruit is fermented 30–60 days in wooden, clay or steel vats, rested, then distilled in an **alambique** — the best of it in a discontinuous, direct-fire still. Legal *aguardente de medronho* runs 38–54% ABV. **Monchique** has a centuries-old tradition of it and a dedicated museum. Monchique is also the address for *presunto* and *enchidos*.

**Produce.** Oranges — **Silves is the largest orange producer in the Algarve**. Almonds, figs, carob (*alfarroba*), and increasingly avocados, tomatoes, cauliflowers, strawberries and raspberries. Almond blossom in the barrocal is a January–February event that people drive out to see.

## Restaurants & institutions

**Mercados de Olhão.** The two red-brick pavilions on the Avenida 5 de Outubro, inaugurated 1915–16 and refurbished in the late 1990s. West pavilion: fish and shellfish — reckoned the best fish market in the Algarve, and it is the argument-settler for the whole region. East pavilion: fruit, vegetables, cheese, enchidos, sweets. Monday to Saturday, roughly 7h00 to 13h00. On **Saturday morning** the market spills outside, when small producers bring in whatever the *horta* gave them that week, and the whole town is there. The ritual is early and unhurried: you go before nine or you go home disappointed, you buy from *the* peixeira rather than *a* peixeira, and you are expected to say what you are cooking so she can tell you that you have chosen wrong and hand you something better. Everybody has a fixed opinion about whether the Olhão counters or the Quarteira lota gives up the better fish, and nobody has ever changed their mind about it.

**Mercado Municipal de Loulé.** The neo-Moorish market hall with its red domes and horseshoe arches — the most photographed civic building in the region and, more to the point, still a working market Monday to Saturday from about half past six. On **Saturday** the market takes over the surrounding streets with producers, plants, cheese, honey and *enchidos*. Saturday in Loulé is a social obligation, not a shopping trip: the standing joke is that the coffee afterwards takes longer than the shopping did.

**Mercado Municipal de Portimão.** Monday to Friday 7h00–14h00 *and* 17h00–20h00, Saturday 7h00–14h00 — the split day is a genuinely useful local detail, and the evening session is when people who work go.

**Mercado Municipal de Faro** — Monday to Friday 8h30–19h00, Saturday 9h00–13h00. **Tavira** — daily except Sundays and holidays, shutting at 15h00. **Silves** — Monday to Saturday 7h00–14h00. **São Brás de Alportel** and **Monte Gordo** — Monday to Saturday 8h00–13h00.

**A lota.** Olhão is the largest fishing port in the Algarve, and the *lota* — the fish auction — is the institution the market depends on. Prices at the counter are downstream of what happened at the lota that morning, and everyone talks as though they know exactly what happened there.

**As churrasqueiras da Guia.** A whole village of charcoal grills on and around the EN125 at Guia, all doing the same small chicken with the same piri-piri, and all considered by somebody to be the only correct one. Ordering is minimal — *um frango, picante* — and the correct accompaniment argument (chips, rice, or both) is settled family by family and never regionally.

**Cervejarias and marisqueiras.** The standing pattern across the region: an *imperial* on the counter, *marisco* ordered by the kilo and priced by the kilo, a paper cloth, and a bill totted up at the end. The reliable comedy is the table that orders "só uma dose para picar" and then quietly orders a second, and the annual argument over whether cataplana is improved or ruined by chouriço.

**Festival do Marisco de Olhão** — the town's own institution, held every **August**, and the one week of the year when Olhão is louder than Albufeira.

## Everyday life & errands

**The season is the calendar.** From June to mid-September the coast is full, the EN125 is a queue, parking in Albufeira and Quarteira is theoretical, and everyone in hospitality works every day. From November to February the same towns are half shut — *encerrado para férias*, *fecha para obras* — the beach bars are boarded, and you can park anywhere. Residents plan dentist appointments, house repairs and holidays around this inversion. Northern-European winter residents (the *andorinhas*, the swallows) arrive in autumn and leave in spring, which keeps the winter cafés alive.

**Markets — which day, which town.** Daily market halls are listed above. The periodic *feiras* are what people mean when they say "vou à feira":

- **Quarteira** — the big weekly market, **every Wednesday, 8h00–14h00**, on Avenida Francisco Sá Carneiro. There are in fact two Wednesday markets in the town, the *mercado semanal de produtores* and the *mercado semanal da Fonte Santa*; both run the same hours, and locals distinguish them without thinking about it.
- **Quarteira** — *feira de antiguidades e velharias*, the **first Saturday of every month, 8h00–13h00**, on the producers' market ground.
- **Portimão** — **Feira de São Martinho**, second week of November, around ten days.
- Beyond these, most villages in the sotavento and the barrocal — Almancil, Moncarapacho, Fuseta, Quelfes, Estói, Boliqueime, Algoz, Monchique — run their own monthly *feira* or *feira de velharias* on a fixed Sunday, Thursday or Monday of the month. The days genuinely differ from village to village and are set by the *junta de freguesia*, so the local habit is to ask, or to check the junta's page, rather than to assume. *"Quando é a feira cá?"* is a normal, useful question and nobody minds being asked it.

**Trains.** The **Linha do Algarve** runs 139.5 km from **Lagos** to **Vila Real de Santo António**, Iberian gauge, stopping at Mexilhoeira Grande, Portimão, Silves, Alcantarilha, Tunes, Albufeira-Ferreiras, Boliqueime, Loulé, Faro, Olhão, Fuzeta-Moncarapacho and Tavira among others. *Regional* services run the whole line; **Alfa Pendular** and **Intercidades** work the Faro–Tunes section, where **Tunes** is the junction onto the Linha do Sul for Lisbon. All traffic on the line is controlled from Faro station. The regional train is slow, cheap, and the standard way to get from Faro to Olhão or Tavira without parking.

**Roads and buses.** The **A22 / Via do Infante** carried electronic-only tolls from **8 December 2011** until they were **abolished on 1 January 2025**, when the whole set of ex-SCUT motorways went free — roughly 951 km nationally. The A22 is free today; anyone still talking about *pagar a portagem na Via do Infante* is talking about the past, and the fourteen-year fight to end it is a live local memory. While the tolls ran, traffic and accidents on the free **EN125** rose sharply, which is why that road is a permanent conversation topic. Regional buses run under the **Vamus Algarve** network.

**Boats are transport, not tourism.** Ferries from Olhão serve **Armona**, **Culatra** and **Farol** in 15–30 minutes; Olhão to Farol is about 45 minutes. **Ilha da Culatra** has around 750 permanent residents in three settlements — Farol, Culatra and Hangares — with a school, an *infantário*, a church, a *posto médico*, *mercearias*, cafés and restaurants, and **no cars at all**: everything, including the shopping and the building materials, comes by boat. *Barcos-táxi* run 24 hours with prior booking. Tavira and Fuseta have their own island boats. At the far east, a ferry crosses the Guadiana from **Vila Real de Santo António to Ayamonte**, and the **Ponte Internacional do Guadiana** carries the A22 into Spain — remember Spain is **one hour ahead**, which is the classic mistake on a shopping trip to Huelva.

**Bureaucracy.** The vocabulary a resident actually needs: **número de contribuinte (NIF)** and the **Autoridade Tributária** (*as Finanças*); **cartão de cidadão** and the **Loja do Cidadão**; **junta de freguesia** for an *atestado de residência*; **câmara municipal** for anything to do with property or licences; **centro de saúde**, **número de utente** and **SNS 24**; **CTT** for post and parcels; **Multibanco** and **MB WAY** for everything else; the car's annual **inspecção (IPO)** and **IMT**. Foreign residents deal with **AIMA** — the Agência para a Integração, Migrações e Asilo — which was created on **29 October 2023**, the day the old **SEF** was extinguished, and which handles residence permits.

**Water and fire.** Rain falls in a short winter window and the region lives off reservoirs; drought (*a seca*) and water restrictions are a recurring, serious, year-round resident topic, argued over alongside golf-course irrigation and the expansion of avocado plantations. Summer is fire season, especially in the Serra de Monchique, which burned badly in 2018; IPMA warnings and *limpeza de terrenos* obligations are part of the annual rhythm for anyone with land.

## Festivals & calendar

- **January–February** — almond blossom (*amendoeiras em flor*) whitens the barrocal; people drive inland to see it.
- **February/March** — **Carnaval de Loulé**, the region's biggest *corso* with *carros alegóricos*, widely described as one of the finest carnival parades in the country.
- **March/April** — **Mãe Soberana**, Loulé. The **Festa Pequena** on **Easter Sunday**, when the image of Nossa Senhora da Piedade comes down from the hilltop sanctuary to the Igreja de São Francisco; the **Festa Grande** **fifteen days later**, when it is carried back up. Thousands walk it.
- **Quinta-feira da Ascensão** — *feriado municipal* of both **Loulé** and **Monchique**.
- **Late May / early June** — **Festa do Pescador**, Quarteira, run by the fishermen's association Quarpesca.
- **June — Santos Populares.** Santo António (13), São João (24), São Pedro (29): *arraiais*, *marchas*, grilled sardines, manjerico, paper decorations. **16 June** is Olhão's *feriado municipal*, marking the 1808 revolt. **24 June** is the *feriado municipal* of **Tavira** and of **Castro Marim**.
- **July** — **Concentração Internacional de Motos de Faro**, the motorcycle rally, held in Faro (42nd edition in 2025, 17–20 July). The town fills with bikes for four days.
- **August** — **Festival do Marisco de Olhão**, the seafood festival. **Feira Medieval de Silves** in and around the castle. **Dias Medievais de Castro Marim**, towards the end of August. Peak of everything else.
- **Summer** — **Festival MED**, Loulé, world music in the old town.
- **27 October** — *feriado municipal* of **Lagos**.
- **November** — **Feira de São Martinho**, Portimão, second week, around ten days. *Castanhas* and *água-pé* everywhere on 11 November.
- **11 December** — *feriado municipal* of **Portimão**.
- **Year-round** — *feiras de velharias* and monthly *mercados* (see above), plus the *festa da freguesia* pattern: every village has its own saint, its own weekend, its own *arraial*.
- Monchique holds annual festivals for its *enchidos* and *presunto*, and events around *medronho*.

## Language notes

Algarvio is a southern dialect, grouped with the central-southern varieties and sharing a lot with Alentejano (and, interestingly, with Madeiran and Azorean speech). Its reputation is for being fast, flat and swallowed — and phonetically that reputation is earned.

**Monophthongisation of diphthongs** is the headline feature: *ei* → *ê*, *ou* → *ô*, *ão* → *ã*, *ãe* → *ẽn*. Attested examples: *manêra* (maneira), *lête* (leite), *ôtro* (outro), *animás* (animais), *chapé* (chapéu), *fêjõs* (feijões), *sês* (seis), **pã** (pão), *papés* (papéis). That *ão* → *ã* is the single most identifiable thing: *não* comes out closer to *nã*, and the plural *-ões* leans toward *-ôns*.

**Apocope** — the final vowel drops off: *fôg* (fogo), *cumpriment* (cumprimento), *fervid* (fervido), *amig* (amigo).

**Paragoge** — an extra *-i* is added after final *-r*: *dizêri* (dizer), *fazêri* (fazer), *dormíri* (dormir).

**Proparoxytone simplification** — the middle of long words disappears: *fenomo* (fenómeno), *triango* (triângulo), *capito* (capítulo), *estômo* (estômago), *arve* (árvore).

**Stressed *ô* preserved** where standard Portuguese opens it: *ôvos*, *jôgos*, *carinhôsa*.

**Liaison with /ʒ/ instead of /z/** — a final *-s* linking into a following vowel comes out as *j*, not *z*: *estás a ver?* is heard as *tájavêr?* rather than *tázavêr?* This is described as the most distinctively Algarvian feature of all.

**Grammar habits**: heavy preference for the gerund and for *a gente* over *nós*; *vocês* for *vós*. The gerund matters and carries a trap: *estou fazendo* here is genuine southern European Portuguese with its own history, **not** a Brazilianism, and it must never be corrected in a local speaker — but a learner should still produce *estar a + infinitivo*, because in a foreign mouth the gerund reads as Brazilian.

Words and expressions (many from Eduardo Brazão Gonçalves, *Dicionário do Falar Algarvio*):

- `barlavento` — the western Algarve, windward
- `sotavento` — the eastern Algarve, leeward
- `açoteia` — the flat roof terrace of an Olhão or Fuseta house

- `conquilha` — the small wedge clam of the local sands
- `alfarroba` — carob
- `medronho` — the arbutus fruit, and the spirit distilled from it
- `medronheiro` — the arbutus tree itself
- `alambique` — the still
- `almadrava` — the traditional fixed-net tuna fishery
- `armação` — the tuna trap installation
- `sapal` — salt marsh
- `esteiro` — a tidal channel in the ria
- `viveiro` — a shellfish nursery bed
- `marnoto` — the salt-pan worker who reads the pans
- `percebeiro` — a goose-barnacle harvester

## Towns

### Faro

**Known for:** the district capital — university, hospital, airport, courts, the *Sé*, and the administrative errands everyone in the region eventually drives in for. About 67,650 in the concelho (2021).
**Landmark:** the **Cidade Velha** behind the **Arco da Vila**, with the **Sé** on the Largo da Sé and the storks on the rooftops; the **Igreja do Carmo** and its **Capela dos Ossos** (1816) a few streets north.
**Food detail:** the Mercado Municipal (Mon–Fri 8h30–19h00, Sat 9h00–13h00), and the Ria Formosa shellfish that arrives with it.
**Everyday life:** Faro is where you go for the Loja do Cidadão, the hospital and the university; it empties on Saturday afternoon and is not a nightlife town. Praia de Faro sits on a sandspit reached by one bridge, which in August is exactly as fun as it sounds.

### Olhão

**Known for:** the largest fishing port in the Algarve, cubic flat-roofed houses with *açoteias*, and the markets. 44,623 residents (2021).
**Landmark:** the two brick market pavilions of 1915–16 on the waterfront; the **Igreja Matriz de Nossa Senhora do Rosário** (1715) with the **Capela de Nosso Senhor dos Aflitos**.
**Food detail:** **xarém com conquilhas** is Olhão's own dish; the **Festival do Marisco** every August.
**Everyday life:** the ferry quay is a commuter terminal — Armona, Culatra, Farol — and Saturday morning at the market is the town's fixed social appointment. Olhão remembers 1808, when the crew of the caíque *Bom Sucesso* sailed to Brazil to tell the Prince Regent the town had risen against the French; the *feriado municipal* is 16 June.

### Tavira

**Known for:** churches, bridges and salt. 13,843 in the town, 27,524 in the concelho (2021). Recognised by UNESCO as a Portuguese representative community of the Mediterranean Diet.
**Landmark:** the **Ponte Antiga** over the Rio Gilão, the castle, and **Santa Maria do Castelo** — 13th century, on the mosque, with the tombs of the seven knights and of D. Paio Peres Correia, who took the town in 1242.
**Food detail:** *atum* and the *almadrava* heritage; salt and *flor de sal* from the surrounding *salinas*.
**Everyday life:** the market runs daily except Sunday and closes at 15h00; the island boat to **Ilha de Tavira** goes from Quatro Águas. *Feriado municipal* 24 June.

### Loulé

**Known for:** the biggest concelho in the Algarve by population (72,348, 2021) and the one that contains both the serra villages and Quarteira, Almancil and Vilamoura.
**Landmark:** the neo-Moorish **Mercado Municipal** with its red domes, the castle, and the **Santuário de Nossa Senhora da Piedade** on the hill.
**Food detail:** Saturday market produce — honey, cheese, *enchidos*, *medronho*, *doces de amêndoa e figo* — and, ten minutes down the road at Almancil, the azulejo church of **São Lourenço**.
**Everyday life:** Saturday morning in Loulé, Wednesday morning at the **Quarteira** market, Carnaval in February, Mãe Soberana at Easter. *Feriado municipal*: Quinta-feira da Ascensão.

### Albufeira

**Known for:** mass tourism and the sharpest seasonal swing in the country. 44,707 residents (2021), of whom roughly 22.5% were born abroad — the most multicultural municipality in Portugal.
**Landmark:** the old town above **Praia dos Pescadores**, reached through the tunnel; the ruined **Castelo de Paderne** inland.
**Food detail:** **Guia**, in the concelho, is the home of the *franguinho* — piri-piri chicken — with a roundabout on the EN125 built to honour it, and local wine besides.
**Everyday life:** the working population is hospitality, and the year has two speeds: everyone works every day from June to September, then half the seafront shuts. Olhos de Água, 5 km east, is still recognisably a fishing settlement.

### Portimão

**Known for:** canning, then tourism. 59,845 residents (2021); the largest urban centre in the barlavento.
**Landmark:** the **Museu de Portimão** in the old **Feu Hermanos** cannery (from 1902), the **Ponte Velha** over the Arade to Ferragudo, and the **Autódromo Internacional do Algarve** (2008).
**Food detail:** **sardinha assada** on the *zona ribeirinha* — grilled outdoors, eaten on bread with roast peppers and boiled potato.
**Everyday life:** the Mercado Municipal opens twice a day (7h00–14h00 and 17h00–20h00 on weekdays), which suits people who work; the **Feira de São Martinho** takes over the town for ten days in November. *Feriado municipal* 11 December.

### Lagos

**Known for:** the walled town at the western end of the railway, the Discoveries, and a working marina and fishing port. Around 33,500 residents (2021).
**Landmark:** the **Igreja de Santo António** with its *talha dourada*; the **Mercado de Escravos** on the Praça do Infante, now a museum of the slave trade; **Ponta da Piedade**.
**Food detail:** **Dom Rodrigos** — fios de ovos, almond, sugar and cinnamon in coloured foil — are Lagos's sweet.
**Everyday life:** Lagos is the western terminus of the Linha do Algarve, so the train is genuinely useful here; **Meia Praia** is the long flat beach residents actually use. *Feriado municipal* 27 October.

### Silves

**Known for:** having been **Xelb**, the Moorish capital of the *al-Gharb*, and now for oranges — the largest orange producer in the Algarve. 38,239 in the concelho (2021).
**Landmark:** the red sandstone **castle**, the **Sé Catedral**, the **Ponte Antiga** over the Arade, and the **Cruz de Portugal**.
**Food detail:** *laranja*, and the citrus economy that runs the surrounding countryside.
**Everyday life:** the market runs Monday to Saturday 7h00–14h00; the **Feira Medieval** fills the whole hill town for several days in summer. Inland Silves is much quieter in winter than the coast and colder than people expect.

### Monchique

**Known for:** the serra — cork, chestnut, eucalyptus, *medronheiro* — and for being the one place in the Algarve where it is properly cold and wet. About 5,323 residents (2021) across 395 km².
**Landmark:** **Fóia** (902 m), the Algarve's highest point; **Caldas de Monchique**, the thermal spa in use since Roman times; the Manueline portal of the Igreja Matriz and the ruined **Convento de Nossa Senhora do Desterro**.
**Food detail:** **aguardente de medronho** distilled in *alambiques* (38–54% ABV), plus *presunto* and *enchidos*, celebrated at annual fairs.
**Everyday life:** basketry and weaving are still made here; the 2018 fire is the reference point for every conversation about summer, and *limpeza de terrenos* is a legal obligation and a neighbourly one. *Feriado municipal*: Quinta-feira da Ascensão.

## Prompt context

The learner lives in the Algarve, southern Portugal — al-Gharb — and speaks European Portuguese only. Their week runs on the *mercado municipal* (Olhão's two brick pavilions from 1915, Loulé's neo-Moorish hall), the Wednesday *feira* in Quarteira, the Saturday market in Loulé and Olhão, and the *feiras de velharias* that rotate through Almancil, Moncarapacho, Fuseta and Estói. They shop for *conquilhas*, *amêijoas*, *atum*, *laranjas de Silves*, *figos*, *amêndoa* and *alfarroba*; they cook *xarém*, *cataplana*, *sardinha assada*, *frango à Guia*; they drink *medronho* from Monchique and eat *dom rodrigos*. Errands mean *as Finanças*, the *junta de freguesia*, the *centro de saúde*, AIMA, Multibanco and MB WAY. Transport means the slow *comboio regional* along the Linha do Algarve, the EN125 and the A22 (Via do Infante), and the ferries across the Ria Formosa to Armona, Culatra and Farol. Life swings between August chaos and a winter when half the coast is *encerrado*. The accent is southern: diphthongs flatten (*pã* for pão, *lête* for leite), final vowels drop, and speech runs fast.
