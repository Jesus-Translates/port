# Bairros de Lisboa

## How Lisbon is organised

Lisbon has two overlapping maps, and residents use both without ever explaining the
difference.

The **official** map is the **freguesia** — the civil parish. Since the 2012
administrative reform the concelho de Lisboa has **24 freguesias**: Ajuda, Alcântara,
Alvalade, Areeiro, Arroios, Avenidas Novas, Beato, Belém, Benfica, Campo de Ourique,
Campolide, Carnide, Estrela, Lumiar, Marvila, Misericórdia, Olivais, Parque das Nações,
Penha de França, Santa Clara, Santa Maria Maior, Santo António, São Domingos de Benfica
and São Vicente. The freguesia is where you go for paperwork — the **junta de freguesia**
issues the atestado de residência, runs the local school transport, and puts on the
Santos Populares. When a form asks *freguesia*, this is the list it wants.

The **lived** map is the **bairro**, and it does not line up. Nobody says "moro em
Misericórdia" — they say "moro no Bairro Alto", or "no Chiado", or "no Príncipe Real",
all three of which are the single freguesia da Misericórdia. **Alfama** and **Baixa** are
both Santa Maria Maior. **Graça** is São Vicente. **Mouraria** straddles Santa Maria Maior
and Arroios. So the honest answer to "where do you live?" is a bairro name, and the answer
on the form is a freguesia name, and a Lisboeta switches between them without noticing.

A few useful consequences for a learner:

- **"Sou de Alvalade"** is an identity claim, not an address. Bairro names carry class,
  age and politics — Campo de Ourique reads *família com filhos*, Marvila reads
  *industrial a mudar*, Alvalade reads *classe média de sempre*.
- **"Vou à Baixa"** does not mean "I am going downtown" in the vague sense. It means the
  Pombaline grid between Rossio/Praça da Figueira and Praça do Comércio — going to the
  bank, the notary, the old shops, the loja da Vodafone, buying something you cannot buy
  in your own bairro. It is an errand phrase. If you are going out to drink you say the
  bairro: *vou ao Cais do Sodré*, *vou ao Bairro Alto*, *vou ao Príncipe Real*.
- Lisbon is built on hills, so residents talk in verbs of altitude. You **sobe** to Graça
  and **desce** to the Baixa; the *zona alta* and the *zona ribeirinha* are real
  distinctions. "É a subir" is a warning, not a direction.
- The city is also read by **metro colour** rather than line number. People say
  *apanha a amarela*, *muda na Alameda*, *é na verde* — never "line 3".
- **A outra banda** (or *o outro lado*) means the south bank of the Tejo — Almada,
  Cacilhas, Seixal. Crossing it is a decision, and the sentence usually includes the
  bridge: *"Vamos pela ponte ou pela Vasco da Gama?"*

**The metro, as residents name it.** Four lines, always by colour:

- **Linha Azul** (Gaivota) — Reboleira ↔ Santa Apolónia. The west-to-riverside line.
- **Linha Amarela** (Girassol) — Odivelas ↔ Rato. The northern spine.
- **Linha Verde** (Caravela) — Telheiras ↔ Cais do Sodré. The line that serves the
  north-central bairros and then dives into the old city.
- **Linha Vermelha** (Oriente) — São Sebastião ↔ Aeroporto. The eastern line, and the one
  you take to the airport.

The long-promised **Linha Circular** — closing Rato to Cais do Sodré with two new stations
at **Estrela** and **Santos** — is **not open**. As of 2026 the Metro is targeting the
first quarter of **2027**, more than three years late. Until then, Estrela has no metro at
all, which shapes daily life there. Do not put a character on a metro at Estrela.

**Trams (eléctricos)** matter as transport, not just as postcards:

- **28E** — Martim Moniz ↔ Graça ↔ Estrela ↔ Campo de Ourique (Prazeres). The famous one.
- **25E** — Praça da Figueira ↔ Campo de Ourique (Prazeres). The one residents actually
  get a seat on.
- **15E** — Praça da Figueira ↔ Belém ↔ Algés. The modern articulated tram to Belém.
- **18E** — Cais do Sodré ↔ Cemitério da Ajuda.
- **12E** — a short clockwise loop from Praça da Figueira past Miradouro de Santa Luzia.
- **24E** — Praça Luís de Camões ↔ Campolide.

And the **ascensores**: the **Glória** (Restauradores up to the Miradouro de São Pedro de
Alcântara), the **Bica** (Rua de São Paulo up to Largo do Calhariz, opened 28 June 1892,
through Rua da Bica de Duarte Belo), and the **Lavra**. The **Elevador de Santa Justa**
lifts you from Rua do Ouro in the Baixa to the level of the Chiado.

## Bairros

### Alfama

- **Character** — The oldest quarter, on the slope between the Castelo and the river, and
  the one the 1755 earthquake largely spared, which is why the street plan is medieval
  rather than Pombaline. Historically a fishermen's and working parish; today a mix of
  long-tenure elderly residents, short-stay rentals and fado. Residents describe it as
  *um bairro a sério* and are quietly proud that it is still lived in. Officially freguesia
  de Santa Maria Maior; the eastern edge runs into São Vicente.
- **Getting there** — No metro inside Alfama. **Santa Apolónia** (linha azul) at the
  bottom, **Terreiro do Paço** (azul) to the west; the **28E** and **12E** trams run the
  upper edge. Most arrivals are on foot, downhill.
- **Landmarks** — The **Sé** (Lisbon cathedral) at the foot; **Miradouro das Portas do
  Sol** and **Miradouro de Santa Luzia**, the two standard meeting points — "encontramo-nos
  nas Portas do Sol" needs no further explanation. The **Panteão Nacional** (Igreja de
  Santa Engrácia) dome is the landmark you steer by from anywhere. **Castelo de São
  Jorge** above. **Campo de Santa Clara** for the market.
- **Food & institutions** — **Feira da Ladra**, on **Campo de Santa Clara**, is the
  institution. It runs **Tuesdays and Saturdays**, morning into afternoon, and has been on
  that square since **1882** (Saturdays were added in **1903**); the fair itself traces
  back to the 13th century and has been evicted from half of Lisbon on its way here. Its
  ritual is the slow lap: you walk the whole thing once without buying, decide what you
  actually want, then come back and find it gone. Haggling is expected but gentle —
  the opening move is a sigh and *"e o menos que faz?"* Alfama's other institution is fado:
  small rooms where the lights go down, someone says **"silêncio, que se vai cantar o
  fado"**, and every fork in the room stops mid-air. A learner should know that clapping
  in the wrong gap is the local equivalent of talking in church, and that everyone has done
  it once.
- **Everyday detail** — There is effectively nowhere to park, so residents with a
  **dístico de residente** guard their spot with genuine devotion and everyone else double
  parks (*em segunda fila*) with the hazards on. Furniture and fridges arrive by hand up
  the *escadinhas*. And in June the whole bairro turns into a grill: Alfama is the classic
  Santo António arraial, and residents rent out balcony space, run a sardine stall outside
  their own front door, and complain fondly for a fortnight afterwards about the smell of
  smoke in the curtains.

### Mouraria

- **Character** — The quarter behind Martim Moniz where Lisbon's Muslim population was
  settled after 1147, and the most genuinely multicultural bairro in the city: Portuguese
  families of several generations alongside Chinese, Bangladeshi, Indian, Nepali, Cape
  Verdean and Brazilian shopkeepers. Steep, dense, and much less polished than Alfama over
  the hill. Split between Santa Maria Maior and Arroios.
- **Getting there** — **Martim Moniz** and **Intendente** (linha verde) sit at its two
  ends; **Rossio** (verde) is a short walk. The **28E** starts at Martim Moniz.
- **Landmarks** — **Largo do Intendente**, transformed from a byword for trouble into one
  of the city's most-used squares; **Praça do Martim Moniz**; **Rua do Benformoso**, the
  long spine of small shops and restaurants; **Rua do Capelão** and **Largo da Severa**;
  the **Centro Comercial da Mouraria** for everyday shopping.
- **Food & institutions** — Mouraria is the accepted **berço do fado** — the cradle. **Maria
  Severa Onofriana** (1820–1846), the first named fadista, lived and died on **Rua do
  Capelão**; the **Casa da Severa** stands on the house tradition assigns to her, rebuilt
  by the architect José Adrião and run in connection with the Museu do Fado. The bairro's
  everyday institution is the tasca lunch: a **prato do dia** chalked on a board, a
  **meia-dose** if you are sensible, and a queue at the door of the best-known places
  before 12h30 that regulars beat by arriving at 12h00 and pretending they always eat
  early. Ordering ritual worth teaching: you do not ask for a menu, you ask **"o que é que
  há hoje?"** and accept the answer.
- **Everyday detail** — The smell changes every twenty metres — grelhados, then cardamom,
  then coffee — and residents genuinely navigate by it. The hill up from Martim Moniz to
  the Costa do Castelo is short and merciless; locals with shopping take the long way round
  and are not embarrassed about it.

### Graça

- **Character** — Up on the ridge above Alfama and Mouraria, in the freguesia de São
  Vicente. A working-class bairro of **vilas operárias** — courtyard blocks of small
  workers' houses — that has become one of the most sought-after addresses in the old city
  without losing the associations, the *coletividades* and the neighbours who have been
  there sixty years. Residents call it a village and mean it.
- **Getting there** — No metro. The **28E** is the bairro's lifeline, up through Largo da
  Graça; buses along the ridge. Everything else is a climb.
- **Landmarks** — **Miradouro da Senhora do Monte**, the highest viewpoint in the bairro,
  and **Miradouro da Graça**, officially the **Miradouro Sophia de Mello Breyner Andresen**
  — the poet lived nearby and it was renamed for her after her death in 2004. **Largo da
  Graça** with the **Igreja e Convento da Graça**. **Vila Sousa**, the largest of the
  workers' vilas, and **Vila Berta**, the most photographed.
- **Food & institutions** — **A Voz do Operário**, founded in the second half of the 19th
  century out of the tobacco workers' movement to teach literacy, still runs a school and
  a hall and is the bairro's civic anchor; "sou da Voz" means something here. In June
  **Vila Berta** is dressed head to foot in paper garlands and becomes one of the best-loved
  arraiais in Lisbon, and Graça fields two stages — one at Largo da Graça, one at the
  miradouro. The ritual is that the decorations go up weeks early, the neighbours argue
  cheerfully about whose balcony is best, and the arraial is judged on the garlands as
  much as on the music.
- **Everyday detail** — The **Calçada da Graça** is the climb that defeats visitors;
  residents time the 28E rather than attempt it with bags. Because the ridge catches the
  wind, Graça is several degrees cooler than the Baixa on an August evening, which is
  precisely why half the city comes up to the miradouro at sunset.

### Baixa

- **Character** — Not really residential — it is the errand district. The **Baixa
  Pombalina** is the grid rebuilt to the Marquês de Pombal's plan after the 1755 earthquake:
  straight streets, uniform façades, and the first large-scale earthquake-resistant
  construction in Europe. Freguesia de Santa Maria Maior. Lisboetas come here for banks,
  notaries, the finanças, and the old shops.
- **Getting there** — **Baixa-Chiado** (azul + verde), **Rossio** (verde), **Terreiro do
  Paço** (azul), **Restauradores** (azul). The best-connected square kilometre in Portugal.
- **Landmarks** — **Praça do Comércio**, which everybody still calls the **Terreiro do
  Paço**; **Rossio** (officially Praça D. Pedro IV); **Praça da Figueira**; **Rua Augusta**
  and its arch; the **Elevador de Santa Justa**. "Encontramo-nos no Rossio" or "debaixo do
  arco" are the default meeting instructions.
- **Food & institutions** — **A Ginjinha**, at **Largo de São Domingos 8**, opened in
  **1840** and has been pouring sour-cherry liqueur from one door ever since. The whole
  transaction is a ritual: you ask **com elas** (with the cherries in the glass) or **sem
  elas**, it is served in a shot glass by one person behind a counter the size of a
  wardrobe, and you drink it standing in the square because there is nowhere else to go.
  The cherry stone is the tourist's problem and the local's badge — regulars park it in the
  cheek without breaking sentence. Two doors of Lisbon argue permanently about whether
  *com elas* is the point or an obstacle. Round the corner, **Confeitaria Nacional** on
  **Praça da Figueira** has been going since **1829**, founded by Balthazar Roiz Castanheiro,
  took a medal at the 1878 Paris Universal Exhibition, and was made supplier to the royal
  house by D. Luís I in **1873**. It is the traditional address for **bolo-rei** at
  Christmas, and the December queue out of the door is an annual Lisbon news item — people
  arrive with a list of who else in the family needs one.
- **Everyday detail** — The Baixa street names are the old trades and residents use them
  as a map: **Rua do Ouro** (goldsmiths — officially Rua Áurea), **Rua da Prata**
  (silversmiths), **Rua dos Sapateiros** (cobblers), **Rua dos Correeiros** (harness
  makers), **Rua dos Bacalhoeiros** (cod merchants). Ask for Rua Áurea and you may get a
  blank look; ask for Rua do Ouro and you get directions.

### Chiado

- **Character** — The elegant shopping and theatre quarter on the slope between the Baixa
  and the Bairro Alto, rebuilt after the great fire of 1988 under Álvaro Siza Vieira.
  Bookshops, cafés, the opera, and expensive flats. Freguesia da Misericórdia. If Baixa is
  the errand, Chiado is the outing.
- **Getting there** — **Baixa-Chiado** (azul + verde) — the station has one exit in the
  Baixa and one up in the Chiado, and the escalator between them is a public lift residents
  use quite shamelessly to avoid the hill. The **28E** passes; the **24E** starts at Praça
  Luís de Camões.
- **Landmarks** — **Largo do Chiado** and **Praça Luís de Camões**, the default meeting
  point for the whole west side of the city. **Teatro Nacional de São Carlos**, the opera
  house. **Igreja e Convento do Carmo**, roofless since 1755. **Armazéns do Chiado**.
- **Food & institutions** — **Café A Brasileira**, **Rua Garrett 120**, opened in **1905**
  selling Brazilian coffee and became the meeting place of the 1920s literary crowd; the
  bronze **Fernando Pessoa** sits at a table on the terrace and has had his photograph taken
  more than any other Portuguese writer. The local ritual is the two-tier price: a **bica ao
  balcão** drunk standing in ninety seconds costs a fraction of the same coffee **na
  esplanada**, and Lisboetas will cheerfully stand at the counter beside a terrace full of
  visitors. **Livraria Bertrand**, founded **1732**, was certified by Guinness in **2011** as
  the oldest bookshop still trading anywhere in the world. And **Manteigaria**, on Rua do
  Loreto, sells pastéis de nata from an open kitchen where a bell is rung when a fresh tray
  comes out — the correct response is to look up, pretend you are not going to have another
  one, and have another one. Canela e açúcar are at the counter; adding cinnamon is a
  matter of firm personal doctrine.
- **Everyday detail** — Chiado is the one place where a Lisboeta will admit to taking the
  metro one stop: from Baixa-Chiado's lower entrance to its upper entrance without ever
  boarding a train, purely for the escalators. It is free, it is legitimate, and it saves
  the climb.

### Bairro Alto

- **Character** — A 16th-century grid of narrow streets laid over the hill above the
  Chiado, freguesia da Misericórdia. Two completely different bairros share one address:
  by day it is quiet, residential and full of grandmothers and print-shop history — this
  was the newspaper district — and after about 22h it becomes the city's oldest going-out
  quarter, with people drinking in the street between tiny bars.
- **Getting there** — **Baixa-Chiado** (azul + verde) then up, or the **Ascensor da Glória**
  from Restauradores, or the **Ascensor da Bica** from Rua de São Paulo up to **Largo do
  Calhariz** — inaugurated **28 June 1892** and running through Rua da Bica de Duarte Belo.
- **Landmarks** — **Miradouro de São Pedro de Alcântara**, at the top of the Glória, the
  standard meeting point. **Largo do Calhariz**, **Praça das Flores** on the Príncipe Real
  side, and the grid streets everyone names directly: **Rua da Atalaia**, **Rua do Diário
  de Notícias**, **Rua da Rosa**, **Rua do Norte**.
- **Food & institutions** — The bairro's institution is a practice rather than a building:
  you buy a drink in a bar the size of a hallway and then leave with it, and the evening
  happens in the street between the bars. Nobody sits down. The other is fado — Bairro Alto
  is where fado moved when it came up in the world, and the small fado houses still work
  the same way, with the singer stepping out from behind the bar. A learner should know
  that a *casa de fados* is not a concert: dinner is served, then the room goes dark, and
  the ritual **"silêncio"** applies.
- **Everyday detail** — The residents' truce is real and precise: bars must stop serving
  outside at a set hour, and at closing time the street empties within minutes as everyone
  migrates downhill to **Cais do Sodré** and the **Rua Nova do Carvalho** (the *rua cor-de-
  rosa*). If you are there at 8pm the bairro looks shut, because it is; the shutters go up
  at ten.

### Príncipe Real

- **Character** — Uphill from the Bairro Alto, freguesia da Misericórdia: 19th-century
  palacetes, antique dealers, design shops, and the centre of Lisbon's LGBT+ life. The most
  self-consciously stylish bairro in the city, and the one where a coffee costs the most
  per square metre of table.
- **Getting there** — **Rato** (linha amarela) is the station; Príncipe Real is the walk up
  from it, or the walk across from Bairro Alto. No station of its own.
- **Landmarks** — The **Jardim do Príncipe Real** (officially Jardim França Borges), built
  round a single enormous **cedro-do-buçaco** whose branches are trained flat on an iron
  frame spanning more than twenty metres, so the tree is a roof you sit under. **Praça das
  Flores** downhill towards São Bento — a small square with a century-old kiosk, and the
  place residents actually take their children. **Embaixada**, the concept galleries inside
  the neo-Moorish **Palacete Ribeiro da Cunha**, with horseshoe arches and a courtyard.
  **Jardim Botânico** of the university on the other side.
- **Food & institutions** — The **Mercado Biológico do Príncipe Real** has run in the
  garden every **Saturday morning, roughly 9h–14h, since 2005**. Its ritual is thoroughly
  Príncipe Real: everybody brings their own cloth bag, everybody knows one particular
  producer by name, and the same conversation about tomatoes happens every week with great
  seriousness. The **quiosque** in the garden is the other institution — Lisbon's kiosk
  culture is real, and you order things you will not find elsewhere: a **mazagran** (cold
  coffee with lemon), a **capilé**, a **groselha** for the children.
- **Everyday detail** — Under the cedro is the only reliable shade in the bairro, so on a
  hot Saturday the benches under it are occupied from about ten in the morning by people
  who have no intention of leaving, and there is an unspoken queueing system that involves
  standing nearby looking patient.

### Estrela

- **Character** — Freguesia da Estrela, west of the centre: green, calm, institutional and
  distinctly bourgeois. The Assembleia da República sits at its edge in **São Bento**, so
  political Lisbon lives and lunches here. Families, embassies, and a lot of dogs.
- **Getting there** — **This is the bairro with no metro.** The planned **Estrela** station
  on the Linha Circular is under construction — the tunnel to Santos is dug, the station is
  going into the old Hospital Militar pharmacy building — but opening is now targeted for
  the **first quarter of 2027**. Until then Estrela runs on the **28E** and **25E** trams and
  on buses, and residents will tell you the metro has been "quase pronto" for years. The
  nearest stations are **Rato** (amarela) uphill and **Cais do Sodré** (verde) far below.
- **Landmarks** — The **Basílica da Estrela**, begun **1779** on the vow of D. Maria I that
  she would build a church if she were given an heir; its white dome is visible across half
  the city and is the navigation point for the whole west side. Directly opposite, the
  **Jardim da Estrela**, with a bandstand, a lake and a playground, is where the bairro
  actually lives. **Palácio de São Bento** (the parliament) and the **Cemitério dos
  Ingleses** with Henry Fielding's grave.
- **Food & institutions** — The Jardim da Estrela's **quiosque** and its **coreto**
  (bandstand) are the institution — the garden has a rhythm the neighbours know by heart:
  pushchairs and grandparents until eleven, dog people at six, and a small crowd for
  whatever is playing on the bandstand at the weekend. The bairro is also the home of the
  most Lisbon of civic rituals, the *volta ao jardim* — a lap of the garden that is
  officially exercise and actually a conversation.
- **Everyday detail** — Because there is no metro, the **28E** at rush hour is a genuine
  commuter tram here, not a tourist ride, and residents have a well-practised technique of
  boarding at the less famous stops. The garden has two sets of gates with different closing
  times, and getting this wrong means a long walk round the railings.

### Campo de Ourique

- **Character** — A flat, orderly grid on the plateau — a rarity in Lisbon — laid out in
  the late 19th century, freguesia de Campo de Ourique. The city's byword for a
  family bairro: everything within walking distance, an unusually high number of children,
  and a fierce local identity. Residents describe it as *uma aldeia dentro da cidade* and
  say it about once a week.
- **Getting there** — **No metro.** The **28E** and the **25E** both terminate at **Campo de
  Ourique (Prazeres)**, which means residents can always get a seat — they board at the
  terminus while everyone else fights for space downhill. Buses otherwise.
- **Landmarks** — **Rua Ferreira Borges** and **Rua Coelho da Rocha** are the two spines.
  The **Cemitério dos Prazeres** at the western end, famous for its funerary architecture
  and its avenues of family mausoleums. **Casa Fernando Pessoa**, the poet's last home, on
  **Rua Coelho da Rocha** — the same street as the market. **Jardim da Parada** (Praça
  Duque de Saldanha... locally just *a Parada*) is the bairro's living room.
- **Food & institutions** — Two, and they define the place. The **Mercado de Campo de
  Ourique**, **Rua Coelho da Rocha 104–106**, opened in **1934** and was reworked in **2013**
  into a hybrid: the original fishmongers, butchers and greengrocers still trade in the
  morning, and the central aisle fills with long shared tables at night. The ritual is
  generational — the older neighbours do the real shopping before eleven and are gone by
  the time the tables fill up, and both halves of the market are quietly convinced they are
  the real one. And **Pastelaria Aloma**, opened on Christmas Eve **1943** and the second
  pastelaria ever to open in the bairro, which won the national **Melhor Pastel de Nata**
  competition and makes them entirely by hand — the pastry is opened by hand and there are
  no industrial machines in the workshop. The consequence is a genuinely serious Lisbon
  argument about who makes the best pastel de nata, in which a Campo de Ourique resident
  will name Aloma before you have finished the question, and there is no known way to end
  the discussion.
- **Everyday detail** — The grid is flat, which is why prams, shopping trolleys and elderly
  residents all end up here, and it is the one bairro where a Lisboeta will say "dá para ir
  a pé" about absolutely everything. Parking is the local sport; the streets around the
  Parada fill by seven in the evening and the *dístico de residente* is the difference
  between a two-minute and a twenty-minute walk home.

### Alvalade

- **Character** — The great planned bairro of mid-century Lisbon, and the most solidly
  middle-class address in the city: doctors, teachers, civil servants, families who have
  been in the same flat for three generations. It is calm, leafy, low-rise and deeply
  unfashionable in a way residents regard as a compliment. Freguesia de Alvalade, which in
  the 2012 reform absorbed the former **Campo Grande** and **São João de Brito** parishes —
  so Campo Grande is now inside Alvalade, which catches people out.
- **Getting there** — **Alvalade** station on the **linha verde**. **Roma** (verde) serves
  the south of the bairro and **Campo Grande** (verde + amarela) the north — it is the
  interchange where half of northern Lisbon changes trains.
- **Landmarks** — **Praça de Alvalade**; **Avenida da Igreja**, the bairro's high street;
  **Avenida de Roma** and **Avenida do Brasil**, the two big axes; the **Igreja de São João
  de Brito**. The whole layout comes from **Faria da Costa**'s plan — the *unidade de
  vizinhança* (neighbourhood unit) idea introduced in **1944**, built out in **eight
  células** from **1952** into the 1960s. Each célula was designed to have its own school,
  church, market and shops within walking distance, and it still works that way.
- **Food & institutions** — Alvalade has **two municipal markets**, which is the single
  most useful thing to know about the bairro. **Mercado de Alvalade Norte** opens
  **terça a sábado, 07:30–14:00** — closed Mondays — and sells flowers, drogaria and
  clothing alongside the food halls, with a gelataria and a cafetaria inside. **Mercado de
  Alvalade Sul**, a *mercado de levante* on **Rua Antero de Figueiredo**, opens **segunda a
  sábado, 07:30–14:00**, nearest metro **Roma**. The ritual is early and unsentimental:
  the serious shopping is done well before eleven, the stallholder knows the family, and
  the correct order is by name and quantity with no preamble — *"ó senhora Rosa, dê-me aí
  um quilo"*. Alvalade is also pastelaria country: every célula has one, everyone has a
  fixed allegiance to theirs, and the phrase *"a minha pastelaria"* means one specific
  counter and one specific person who starts making your galão when you walk in.
- **Everyday detail** — The two markets close on different days, and getting it wrong is a
  wasted trip: Norte is shut on Mondays, Sul is not. There is a **paid car park at the
  Mercado de Alvalade Norte** — that is where residents park for the market rather than
  circling the avenues.

### Areeiro

- **Character** — A compact, handsome, mostly 1940s bairro between Alvalade and the older
  city, freguesia de Areeiro (created in 2012 from São João de Deus and most of the old
  Alto do Pina). Residential, well-served, and architecturally the showpiece of the
  *Português Suave* style — the Estado Novo's monumental-but-domestic idiom.
- **Getting there** — **Areeiro** station on the **linha verde**. **Roma** (verde) and
  **Alameda** (verde + vermelha) are on its borders, which makes Areeiro unusually easy to
  reach from anywhere.
- **Landmarks** — The square, officially **Praça Francisco Sá Carneiro** and universally
  called **Praça do Areeiro**: laid out to a **1938** plan by **João Faria da Costa**, with
  the surrounding buildings by **Luís Cristino da Silva**. The **Fonte Luminosa** on the
  Alameda below; the **Igreja de São João de Deus**; the **Instituto Superior Técnico** by
  Pardal Monteiro. Avenida de Roma, Avenida Almirante Reis and Avenida Guerra Junqueiro run
  off it.
- **Food & institutions** — **Pastelaria Mexicana**, **Avenida Guerra Junqueiro 30**, is
  the institution and one of the loveliest interiors in Lisbon. It opened in **1946** and
  was remodelled in **1961–62** by the modernist architect **Jorge Ferreira Chaves**; it was
  classified a **Monumento de Interesse Público in 2014**. Inside, intact from the sixties:
  the **"Sol Mexicano" ceramic panel by Querubim Lapa**, the original interior telephone
  booth, and a glass **birdcage with live birds** — which means you take your galão to the
  sound of birdsong under a ceramic sun, in a room where Surrealist and Neo-Realist painters
  and modernist architects used to argue. The ritual is that regulars have a table and
  visitors have the counter, and everyone under the birdcage lowers their voice slightly
  without knowing why.
- **Everyday detail** — Nobody says "Praça Francisco Sá Carneiro" out loud. It is *Praça do
  Areeiro* in speech and *Areeiro* on the metro tile, and this gap between the official name
  and the spoken one is the single most Lisbon thing about the bairro. (The **Bairro dos
  Actores**, with its Rua Actor Isidoro and Rua Actriz Virgínia, is often filed under Areeiro
  but sits closer to Alameda on the Arroios side — treat it as a border case *(unverified
  which side)*.)

### Benfica

- **Character** — A large, green, self-contained bairro out to the north-west, freguesia de
  Benfica: old village spine, mid-century apartment blocks, and an enormous amount of
  forest. Around 35 000 residents. It feels less like central Lisbon and more like a town
  that the city grew around, and residents rather like that.
- **Getting there** — **There is no metro station called Benfica.** The nearest are
  **Colégio Militar/Luz** and **Alto dos Moinhos** on the **linha azul**, with Carnide,
  Pontinha and Alfornelos continuing north-west on the same line. This is the sort of thing
  that trips up newcomers, and it is worth teaching directly.
- **Landmarks** — **Estrada de Benfica**, the old spine and still the commercial street,
  carrying the **Igreja de Nossa Senhora do Amparo**, the **Chafariz de Benfica**, and the
  workers' vilas **Vila Ana** and **Vila Ventura**. **Parque Silva Porto**, which everyone
  calls the **Mata de Benfica**, in the Bairro de Santa Cruz. And about **two-thirds of the
  Parque Florestal de Monsanto**, the city's green lung, lies inside Benfica.
- **Food & institutions** — The **Mercado de Benfica**, beside the Estrada de Benfica, is
  the bairro's market *(opening days unverified)*. Benfica's other institution is not
  edible: it is football, and specifically the fact that a Benfiquista from Benfica
  considers this a meaningful coincidence and will tell you so. The ritual around a match
  is the whole point — the café fills an hour early, the volume goes up, the same three men
  have the same argument about the coach, and afterwards nobody goes home quickly.
- **Everyday detail** — Almost nothing named "Benfica" is actually in Benfica. The
  **Estádio da Luz** is in the neighbouring freguesia of **São Domingos de Benfica**
  (Avenida Eusébio da Silva Ferreira), and so is the **Palácio dos Marqueses de Fronteira**
  with its azulejo Sala das Batalhas. The two parishes split in **1959** and still share
  streets. Benfica proper is the Estrada de Benfica corridor plus its share of Monsanto,
  and a resident will make this distinction unprompted.

### Telheiras

- **Character** — Two bairros in one name. **Telheiras Velho** is the old settlement along
  the **Estrada de Telheiras** — an 18th- and 19th-century core of quintas and a former
  convent. Around it sits one of the most deliberately designed neighbourhoods in Portugal:
  the **Plano Urbanístico de Telheiras** by the architect **Pedro Vieira de Almeida**, drawn
  in the **1970s**, roughly **3 300 dwellings** across **17 "células"**, threaded with
  pedestrian paths and with the schools placed at the *corners* between cells rather than in
  the middle of them. The result is quiet, green, walkable and much loved by the families
  who live there. Note that Telheiras is **not a freguesia** — the bairro is split between
  **Lumiar** and **Carnide**.
- **Getting there** — **Telheiras** station, **linha verde** — and it is the **northern
  terminus** of the line.
- **Landmarks** — **Praça Central**, the planned bairro's square; the **Biblioteca Orlando
  Ribeiro** (opened 2003); the **Igreja Paroquial de Nossa Senhora da Porta do Céu**, a
  17th-century former convent founded by Prince João de Cândia, whose marble tomb is still
  inside; and the **Estrada de Telheiras** through the old core.
- **Food & institutions** — No municipal market and no long-standing named pastelaria or
  tasca could be verified for Telheiras *(unverified — omitted rather than guessed)*. What
  is certain is the shape of daily life: the pedestrian paths mean the bairro's social life
  happens on foot, and the esplanadas around Praça Central function as the village square —
  school run at half past eight, coffee at nine, the same faces.
- **Everyday detail** — Being the terminus is a genuine daily advantage: southbound trains
  **start empty at Telheiras**, so residents board with a seat while the rest of the linha
  verde fights for standing room. The other quirk is the street plan — it is cellular and
  deliberately not through-routed, so you cannot drive straight across Telheiras. You go
  round. Visitors with satnavs learn this the hard way.

### Lumiar

- **Character** — The largest of the northern freguesias by population — about **46 000
  residents** across **6,57 km²** — and a genuine patchwork: the old aristocratic village
  of **Paço do Lumiar** with its walled quintas, mid-century apartment blocks, and the big
  modern developments of the Alta de Lisboa. Green, spacious, and family-heavy.
- **Getting there** — **Lumiar** and **Quinta das Conchas** stations on the **linha amarela**,
  with **Campo Grande** (amarela + verde) and **Telheiras** (verde) on its edges.
- **Landmarks** — **Quinta das Conchas e dos Lilases**, the big park where the whole
  north-west of the city walks its dogs. **Parque do Monteiro-Mor**, which contains the
  **Palácio do Monteiro-Mor**, the **Museu Nacional do Traje**, the **Museu Nacional do
  Teatro e da Dança** and the **Jardim Botânico do Lumiar**. The **Paço do Lumiar** historic
  core, with the **Quinta dos Azulejos**, the **Quinta das Hortênsias**, the **Quinta do
  Marquês de Angeja** and the **Capela de São Sebastião**. **Largo de São João Baptista** is
  the old village square.
- **Food & institutions** — No municipal market or historic named pastelaria could be
  verified in Lumiar *(unverified — omitted rather than guessed)*. The bairro's shared
  institution is the park: Quinta das Conchas on a Sunday is a Lisbon ritual in itself —
  families arrive with folding chairs and a cooler, claim a patch of shade by about eleven,
  and stay until the light goes.
- **Everyday detail** — Two things residents know. First, **Monteiro-Mor is one place, not
  four** — the palace, both national museums and the botanical garden share a single
  contiguous park, so you go once and do the lot, and locals are mildly amused by visitors
  who plan them as separate outings. Second, and this is the great Lisbon trick question:
  the **Estádio José Alvalade is in Lumiar**, not in Alvalade.

### Olivais

- **Character** — Eastern Lisbon, and the city's great experiment in planned social
  housing. Rural until the transformation of the **1960s**, when Olivais Norte and Olivais
  Sul were built as modernist estates in open green space — towers and slabs set in lawns,
  a model that architects still come to study. In the 2012 reform the freguesia **lost about
  a quarter of its territory**, the riverside Expo '98 zone, to the new Parque das Nações.
  It remains a mixed, unshowy, strongly rooted bairro.
- **Getting there** — All on the **linha vermelha**: **Olivais**, **Cabo Ruivo**,
  **Oriente**, **Moscavide**, **Encarnação** and **Aeroporto**, with Chelas and Bela Vista
  on the inland edge. Six red-line stations makes Olivais one of the best-connected
  freguesias in the city.
- **Landmarks** — The **Igreja de Santa Maria dos Olivais**, rebuilt after 1755 and the
  origin of the parish's old name. **Praça da Viscondessa dos Olivais**, which was simply
  called *o Rossio* until 1896. **Praça José Queirós**, **Praça Baden-Powell**, **Largo de
  Beirolas**, **Largo do Leão**. The named sub-bairros are how residents actually locate
  themselves: Olivais Norte, Olivais Sul, Olivais Este and the **Centro Histórico dos
  Olivais**, Olivais Oeste, Quinta do Morgado, Quinta da Portela, Cabo Ruivo, and the
  **Bairro da Encarnação**.
- **Food & institutions** — No municipal market could be verified in Olivais *(unverified)*.
  The everyday institution is the **café de bairro** at the foot of the towers, with plastic
  chairs outside and a television permanently on — the estates were designed with ground-
  floor commerce and that is exactly what filled it. The ritual is the *cafezinho* on the way
  back from the school gate, the same table, the same hour.
- **Everyday detail** — Directions here are given by the **Rotunda do Relógio** — the local
  name for **Praça do Aeroporto** — and if you say "Praça do Aeroporto" you will get a pause
  before the answer. Olivais is also the one Lisbon bairro where *"moro ao pé do
  aeroporto"* is not an exaggeration: a large part of the freguesia's western territory is
  literally the **Aeroporto de Lisboa**, TAP's head office included. Residents no longer
  hear the planes and will tell you so with some pride.

## Lisbon everyday language

Café and counter:

- `uma bica` — an espresso, in Lisbon. (The northern equivalent is *um cimbalino*.)
- `um café` — the same thing; both are ordered constantly.
- `ao balcão` — standing at the counter, where it is cheapest and fastest.
- `na esplanada` — at an outside table, where the same coffee costs more; everyone knows
  and nobody complains.
- `uma imperial` — a small draught beer, Lisbon and the south. Ask for *um fino* and you
  have announced you are from Porto.
- `uma caneca` — the larger draught beer.
- `um garoto` — espresso with a splash of milk.
- `uma meia de leite` — coffee with milk, in a cup.
- `um galão` — coffee with milk, in a tall glass; the standard breakfast.
- `um abatanado` — a longer, weaker black coffee.
- `um pingado` — a bica with a drop of milk.
- `um quiosque` — the kiosk in a garden or square, a Lisbon institution in its own right.
- `um mazagran` — cold coffee with lemon, a kiosk drink.
- `uma groselha` — redcurrant cordial; the children's kiosk order.
- `um manjerico` — the pot of basil given at Santo António, with a paper carnation and a
  rhyming verse stuck in it.

Eating out:

- `uma tasca` — a small, cheap, unpretentious restaurant. The compliment is *tasca a sério*.
- `uma casa de pasto` — the older word for the same thing.
- `uma cervejaria` — a beer hall, in Lisbon strongly associated with shellfish.
- `uma marisqueira` — a shellfish restaurant.
- `uma pastelaria` — the neighbourhood café-bakery; the real civic institution of any bairro.
- `o prato do dia` — the day's dish, chalked on a board; the correct way to order lunch.
- `uma dose` / `uma meia-dose` — a full or half portion; halves are normal and unembarrassing.
- `os salgados` — savoury snacks: *rissol*, *croquete*, *pastel de bacalhau*, *empada*.
- `uma bifana` — a pork steak in a bread roll. `um prego no pão` — the beef version.
- `um pastel de nata` — the custard tart, everywhere. `um pastel de Belém` — only the ones
  made in Belém; a Lisboeta will correct you on this.
- `ginjinha` — the sour-cherry liqueur. `com elas` — with the cherries in the glass;
  `sem elas` — without.
- `sardinha assada` — grilled sardine, the smell of Lisbon in June.

Streets and buildings — Lisbon has an unusually rich vocabulary for "small street":

- `um largo` — a small square, wider than a street.
- `uma travessa` — a short cross-street.
- `um beco` — a dead-end alley.
- `as escadinhas` — the little stairs; half of Alfama's addresses.
- `uma calçada` — a steep paved street (also *calçada portuguesa*, the black-and-white
  mosaic pavement).
- `um pátio` — an inner courtyard with houses round it.
- `uma vila` — a courtyard block of small workers' houses (*vila operária*).
- `um prédio gaioleiro` — the fast-built late-19th/early-20th-century Lisbon apartment block.
- `o saguão` — the internal light-well; where you hear the neighbours.
- `a marquise` — the glazed-in balcony where the washing goes.
- `um miradouro` — a viewpoint terrace, and a social institution.

Getting about:

- `o elétrico` — the tram. Residents say `o 28`, `o 25`, `o 15`.
- `o ascensor` / `o elevador` — the funicular (Glória, Bica, Lavra).
- `a paragem` — the stop. `a carreira` — the bus route.
- `o passe Navegante` — the monthly transport pass.
- `a Carris` — the bus and tram operator. `a CP` — the trains.
- `o cacilheiro` — the ferry across the Tejo to Cacilhas; *apanhar o cacilheiro*.
- `a outra banda` — the south bank.
- `a ponte` — always the Ponte 25 de Abril; the other one is named in full.
- `a Segunda Circular` — the ring road, and a permanent topic of conversation.
- `a Marginal` — the coast road out to Cascais.
- `a EMEL` — the municipal parking authority.
- `o dístico de residente` — the resident's parking permit; a genuine quality-of-life issue.
- `estacionar em segunda fila` — to double-park, hazards on, "só um bocadinho".

Identity and talk:

- `alfacinha` — a native of Lisbon, from *alface*, lettuce. *Alfacinha da gema* is one
  born of Lisbon-born parents.
- `um bairro` — the lived neighbourhood. `uma freguesia` — the administrative parish.
- `a junta` — the junta de freguesia, where the paperwork happens.
- `uma coletividade` — the local social/sports/cultural association; the backbone of a bairro.
- `um arraial` — the street party.
- `as marchas populares` — the June parades.
- `os Santos Populares` — the June festivals as a whole.
- `subir` / `descer` — to go up / down; the default verbs of movement in this city.
- `bué` — a lot, very (of Angolan Portuguese origin, thoroughly Lisbon).
- `bazar` — to leave, to clear off: *vou bazar*.
- `um cota` — an older person, a parent.
- `fixe` — cool, good.
- `ganda` — great, big, as an intensifier: *ganda cena*.
- `ó pá` — the all-purpose Lisbon vocative.
- `tá-se` / `tá-se bem` — "it's fine", "we're good".
- `bora lá` — let's go.
- `dar um pulo a` — to pop over to somewhere.
- `fazer horas` — to kill time before something.
