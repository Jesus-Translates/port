# Zona: Norte

## Identity

The Norte is where Portugal began and it never lets anyone forget it. Guimarães carries the
inscription *Aqui nasceu Portugal* on the old town wall. Braga was Roman **Bracara Augusta**,
founded 16 BC, and its cathedral — consecrated on 28 August 1089 — is older than the country
itself; the parents of the first king, D. Henrique de Borgonha and D. Teresa de Leão, are buried
in it. Porto is **a Invicta**, "the unconquered", and its people are **tripeiros**, a nickname
they wear proudly.

The old saying sums up how the country sees itself: *Braga reza, Porto trabalha, Coimbra
estuda, Lisboa diverte-se* — Braga prays, Porto works, Coimbra studies, Lisbon enjoys itself.
Northerners are, by reputation and largely in practice, direct, hospitable, hard-working,
loud at table, and quietly convinced that Lisbon gets too much of everything. The rivalry with
the capital is real and constant, and it surfaces in football, in politics, in complaints about
roads and hospitals, and in the way a nortenho pronounces the word *lisboeta*.

Religion is practised here, not just inherited. Parish life, the **romaria**, the **banda
filarmónica** and the **rancho folclórico** are living institutions, not folklore exhibits.
Granite is the default building material — grey, heavy, damp-stained — and it gives every town
from Porto to Bragança a similar dark, solid texture.

The region is really three countries stitched together. The **Minho** (Braga, Viana do Castelo,
Barcelos, Ponte de Lima) is green, wet, densely populated and cut into tiny fields. The **Douro**
is a hot schist gorge terraced by hand into **socalcos** for wine. **Trás-os-Montes** — Bragança,
Mirandela, Miranda do Douro — is high, cold, empty and emptying, a plateau of stone villages,
chestnut trees and smoked meat.

Emigration shapes everything. Whole villages in the Minho and Trás-os-Montes sent people to
France, Luxembourg and Switzerland from the 1960s onwards. Those families come home in August;
the villages triple in population, weddings and **festas** are scheduled around them, and cars
with French plates line the roads. (unverified as a formally named phenomenon, but universally
observed and constantly reported in Portuguese media)

## Geography & climate

This is the wet corner of Portugal. The Atlantic hits the Minho coast, the mountains squeeze
the rain out, and the coastal north gets substantially more precipitation than anywhere south of
the Douro. Braga's wettest month is October, averaging around 220 mm. Porto's winters are mild
and damp rather than cold — grey, drizzly, with the sea close enough to bring fog up the river.
Summers on the coast are warm rather than fierce, and the Atlantic water stays cold all year.
Everyone owns a **guarda-chuva** and everyone complains about the rain; *está a chover outra vez*
is the default winter greeting.

Inland, the picture changes fast. The **Douro valley** around Peso da Régua and Pinhão is
sheltered by the Serra do Marão, and its dark schist soils absorb and radiate heat — summers
there are genuinely punishing, and the terraced vineyards bake.

**Trás-os-Montes** is continental, not Atlantic. It splits into two traditional halves:

- **Terra Fria** — the high plateaux exposed to north-easterly winds, very cold in winter and
  very hot in summer. This is the country of chestnuts (**castanhas**), walnuts and rye
  (**centeio**).
- **Terra Quente** — the lower, sheltered schist valleys, where temperatures climb
  extraordinarily high; olives and almonds.

The Terra Fria is where the proverb *nove meses de inverno e três de inferno* — nine months of
winter and three of hell — is used. It is an exaggeration, and locals say it with a grin, but it
captures the place.

Mountains and rivers a resident names constantly: the **Serra do Gerês** and **Serra da Peneda**
(the **Parque Nacional da Peneda-Gerês**, created in 1971, is Portugal's only national park; it
covers Terras de Bouro, Melgaço, Arcos de Valdevez, Ponte da Barca and Montalegre, and shelters
the wild **garranos**, the small native ponies); the **Serra do Marão** between Amarante and Vila
Real; the **Parque Natural de Montesinho** above Bragança. Rivers: the **Minho** (the Spanish
border), the **Lima**, the **Cávado**, the **Ave**, the **Tâmega**, the **Sabor**, the **Tua** and
the **Douro** itself.

In the mountain villages of Soajo and Lindoso stand the **espigueiros** — raised stone granaries
on stilts, topped with crosses. Lindoso has the largest surviving cluster in the Iberian
Peninsula.

## Landmarks & buildings

**Porto.** The **Torre dos Clérigos**, 75 metres of Nasoni's baroque built between 1754 and 1763,
is the thing you navigate by. **Estação de São Bento**, opened 1916 on the site of a Benedictine
monastery, has an atrium lined with roughly twenty thousand **azulejos** painted by Jorge Colaço
between 1905 and 1908 — the battle of Valdevez, the conquest of Ceuta, the arrival of D. João I
and Filipa de Lencastre, rural harvest scenes. **Livraria Lello**, the **Palácio da Bolsa**, the
**Mercado Ferreira Borges**, Rem Koolhaas's **Casa da Música** at Boavista and Siza Vieira's
**Museu de Serralves** are the other set pieces. The **Ponte Luís I** carries the metro on its
upper deck and cars and pedestrians below; the **Ponte da Arrábida**, the **Ponte do Infante**,
the disused Eiffel-era **Ponte Maria Pia** and the rail **Ponte de São João** complete the
crossings. The **Ribeira** is the riverfront; **Foz do Douro** is where the river meets the sea
and where Porto goes to walk.

**Vila Nova de Gaia.** The **caves** — the port wine lodges — line the south bank, with the
**Cais de Gaia** in front of them, a **teleférico** running up the hillside, and the
**Mosteiro da Serra do Pilar** with its circular cloister looking straight across at Porto.

**Braga.** The **Arcada** and the **Avenida da Liberdade** are the civic centre; the
**Theatro Circo** is the theatre everyone knows; the **Universidade do Minho** fills the city
with students; and the ruins of Bracara Augusta surface all over the old town.

**Guimarães.** The **Castelo de Guimarães**, founded in the tenth century by Mumadona Dias; the
**Paço dos Duques de Bragança**, built 1420–1433 for D. Afonso, first Duke of Bragança; the
**Largo da Oliveira** with the **Padrão do Salado**; and the **teleférico da Penha** up to the
hilltop above the city. The historic centre is UNESCO-listed and the city was European Capital
of Culture in 2012.

**Viana do Castelo.** The **Santuário de Santa Luzia** on the hill, reached by a funicular from
near the railway station; the hospital ship **Gil Eannes**, built in the town's own
**estaleiros navais** to support the cod fleet and now moored as a museum; the shipyards
themselves, still a major employer.

**Bragança.** The walled **Cidadela** on its hill, with the castle keep and the twelfth-century
**Domus Municipalis** — the only surviving example of Romanesque civil architecture of its kind
in Portugal, a cistern with a vaulted council chamber above it.

**Amarante.** The **Ponte de São Gonçalo** over the Tâmega with the church and convent beside it,
and the **Museu Amadeo de Souza-Cardoso**.

**Barcelos.** The **Torre da Porta Nova**, the ruined **Paço dos Condes**, and the enormous
**Campo da República** that fills with the weekly fair.

**The Douro.** The **socalcos** — terraces — and the **quintas** with their whitewashed names
painted huge on the hillside; the tiled station at Pinhão; the dams that turned the rapids into
a navigable river.

## Churches & religious sites

**Santuário do Bom Jesus do Monte**, Braga — inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage list in 2019.
The baroque **escadório** climbs the hillside in three flights: the **Escadório do Pórtico**, the
**Escadório dos Cinco Sentidos** (each landing a fountain for one of the senses) and the
**Escadório das Três Virtudes**, 581 steps in all, up to the **Terreiro de Moisés** and the
church. Alongside runs the **Elevador do Bom Jesus**, inaugurated in 1882 to a design by Niklaus
Riggenbach — the oldest water-counterweight funicular in the world and the first funicular built
in the Iberian Peninsula: the upper cabin fills its tank, the brake is released, and gravity does
the rest. For Braga residents this is not a monument, it is a Sunday. People drive or take the
funicular up, walk the escadório for exercise or for a promise, sit under the trees, eat, get
married and christen their children there. The **Santuário do Sameiro** is on the next hill and
draws its own pilgrimages.

**Sé de Braga** — the oldest cathedral in Portugal, consecrated 28 August 1089, Romanesque at the
core with Gothic, Renaissance and baroque layered on, and two working eighteenth-century organs
facing each other across the nave. It is the seat of an archbishopric that has run northern
Portugal's religious life for fifteen centuries, and it is where the city's big liturgical
moments happen.

**Semana Santa de Braga** is the north's most serious Holy Week. The **farricocos** — barefoot
penitents in black hooded tunics — lead the **Procissão do Ecce Homo** on Maundy Thursday night
carrying **fogaréus** and rattling **matracas**; the **Senhor dos Passos** procession goes out on
Palm Sunday; the **Enterro do Senhor** on Good Friday is the most solemn of all. It was
officially declared of tourist interest in 2011, but for locals it is a family obligation.

**Porto.** The **Sé do Porto** sits fortress-like above the Ribeira on the Terreiro da Sé. The
azulejo churches are a genre of their own: the **Capela das Almas** on Rua de Santa Catarina,
faced in around sixteen thousand blue tiles painted by Eduardo Leite in 1929 with scenes from
the lives of São Francisco de Assis and Santa Catarina; the **Igreja do Carmo**, whose entire
side wall is a single tile panel; the **Igreja de Santo Ildefonso** on Praça da Batalha, tiled by
Jorge Colaço; the **Igreja de São Francisco**, a Gothic shell whose interior is smothered in
**talha dourada**, gilded carved wood; and the **Igreja dos Clérigos** at the foot of its tower.

**Elsewhere.** The **Santuário de Santa Luzia** above Viana; the **Santuário da Penha** above
Guimarães; the **Mosteiro de Tibães** outside Braga; the **Mosteiro/Igreja de São Gonçalo** in
Amarante; **Nossa Senhora dos Remédios** at Lamego at the top of its own vast staircase, in the
Douro. The **Caminho Português de Santiago** runs north through Porto, Barcelos, Ponte de Lima
and Valença, and residents of those towns see pilgrims with backpacks all summer.

Everyday religious vocabulary matters more than architecture here: **a missa** on Sunday morning,
**a paróquia**, **o pároco**, **a catequese** on Saturday mornings, **o adro** in front of the
church where everyone stands and talks afterwards, **um baptizado**, **um casamento**,
**um funeral**, **a procissão**, and the parish **festa** with its **arraial** and **foguetes**.

## Food & drink

**Porto's own dishes.**

**Francesinha** — the city's emblem. Slices of **pão de forma** layered with **linguiça**,
**salsicha fresca**, **fiambre** and a **bife** or **lombo de porco**, sealed under melted cheese
and drowned in a hot, dense sauce built on tomato, beer and **piri-piri**, with **batata frita**
on the side. Ask for a **francesinha especial** and it arrives with a fried egg on top. It was
created around 1952–53 by Daniel David Silva, a cook from Terras de Bouro who had worked in
France and Belgium, at the restaurant **A Regaleira** on Rua do Bonjardim — a Portuguese
re-engineering of the croque-monsieur. Póvoa de Varzim makes its own **francesinha poveira** on
long baguette-style bread. Every family has an opinion about whose sauce is best.

**Tripas à moda do Porto** — veal tripe slow-cooked with **mão de vitela**, **chouriço**,
**toucinho**, chicken and white beans, seasoned with cumin and bay, served with plain white rice.
The legend ties it to 1415, when Porto handed over all its meat to provision the fleet sailing to
conquer Ceuta and kept only the offal — hence **tripeiros**. It is a heavy, ceremonial,
older-generation dish, cooked at home on Sundays and served in the old **tascas**.

**Bacalhau.** **Bacalhau à Gomes de Sá** is Porto's — flaked salt cod with onion, olive oil,
garlic, sliced potato, black olives and hard-boiled egg. Braga's is **bacalhau à Braga**, also
called **à Narcisa**: thick **postas** fried in plenty of olive oil on a bed of thinly sliced
fried potatoes under a mound of onion. On **Consoada**, Christmas Eve, the north eats
**bacalhau cozido** with potatoes, **couves** and chickpeas, dressed with olive oil and vinegar,
followed by **rabanadas**, **aletria**, **sonhos** and **bolo-rei**.

**Minho cooking** is pork, cabbage and green wine. **Rojões à minhota** — chunks of pork
marinated in **vinho verde** and garlic, then slow-fried in their own fat. **Papas de sarrabulho**
— a thick winter porridge of bread, pork and pig's blood, made at the **matança do porco** and
served with the rojões. **Arroz de cabidela**, called **arroz de pica no chão** in the Minho —
chicken rice finished with the bird's blood. **Caldo verde** — the soup of the region and
arguably of the country: potato purée, finely shredded **couve galega**, a slice of **chouriço**,
a thread of olive oil, and **broa de milho** on the side. **Cabrito assado no forno** for feast
days. **Vitela barrosã** from the Barroso uplands.

**Trás-os-Montes** is smoke and beef. **Posta mirandesa** — a thick steak from the native
**raça mirandesa**, grilled rare, dressed with nothing but **sal grosso** and served with
**batata a murro**. **Alheira de Mirandela** — the horseshoe-shaped sausage of poultry, game and
bread rather than pork, born among Jewish families disguising their diet, and now eaten fried
with an egg and chips everywhere in Portugal. **Butelo com casulas** — a fat smoked sausage
cooked with dried bean pods, celebrated at a food festival in Bragança in mid-February. **Folar
de Valpaços**, a bread stuffed with cured meats, eaten at Easter and sold year-round. The
**fumeiro** in general — **chouriça**, **salpicão**, **presunto** — with winter **feiras do
fumeiro** across the region. And **castanhas**, roasted, boiled, or in stews.

**Coast.** Matosinhos grills fish in the open on **Rua Heróis de França**, beside the **lota**
(the auction hall): **robalo**, **dourada**, **sardinhas**, **polvo**, whatever came in. On the
night of **São João** the whole of Porto grills **sardinhas assadas** in the street.

**Bread and sweets.** **Broa de milho** — dense yellow maize bread — is the northern loaf.
**Regueifa** is the ring-shaped wheat bread; a **molete** is what a Porto person calls a roll.
Amarante's **doces conventuais**, from the recipes of the Convento de Santa Clara, are
**foguetes**, **lérias**, **brisas do Tâmega**, **papos de anjo** and the frankly phallic
**bolos de São Gonçalo**, banned as obscene around 1926 and openly made again after 1974.
Confeitaria da Ponte in Amarante, open since 1930, is the classic address. In Viana do Castelo
people queue at **Confeitaria Manuel Natário** for **bolas de Berlim**.

**Drink.**

- **Vinho verde** — a region, not a style, demarcated on 18 September 1908 and the largest
  denomination in Portugal. Nine sub-regions: **Monção e Melgaço**, **Lima**, **Basto**,
  **Cávado**, **Ave**, **Amarante**, **Baião**, **Sousa**, **Paiva**. **Alvarinho** is the great
  white of Monção e Melgaço; **Loureiro** belongs to the Lima valley; **Vinhão** is the red grape
  with red pulp that makes the near-black, sharp **verde tinto** poured cold into a **tigela** or
  **malga** and drunk with rojões. Ordinary households drink verde as the everyday table wine.
- **Vinho do Porto** — from the Douro, demarcated in 1756, the oldest regulated wine region in
  the world; aged in the **caves** of Vila Nova de Gaia. **Branco** chilled as an
  **aperitivo**, **tawny** and **ruby** after dinner, plus **LBV**, **colheita** and **vintage**.
  The **Alto Douro Vinhateiro** has been UNESCO World Heritage since 2001.
- **Um cimbalino** is what you order in Porto for an espresso (after the old La Cimbali machines);
  **um fino** is a small draught beer — what Lisbon calls an **imperial**. **Super Bock** is the
  northern beer of habit.

## Everyday life & errands

**The Porto metro.** Six lines, about 85 stations, running roughly 06:00 to 01:00, all meeting at
**Trindade**:

- **A (azul)** — Senhor de Matosinhos ↔ Estádio do Dragão
- **B (vermelha)** — Póvoa de Varzim ↔ Estádio do Dragão
- **C (verde)** — ISMAI ↔ Campanhã
- **D (amarela)** — Hospital de São João ↔ Vila d'Este, crossing the upper deck of the Ponte
  Luís I into Gaia; extended past Santo Ovídio to Manuel Leão, Hospital Santos Silva and
  Vila d'Este on 28 June 2024
- **E (violeta)** — Trindade ↔ Aeroporto (Francisco Sá Carneiro)
- **F (laranja)** — Senhora da Hora ↔ Fânzeres

The **Linha Rosa** (G), 2.7 km from São Bento to Casa da Música via Hospital de Santo António and
Galiza, is expected around 2027, and the **Linha Rubi** (H) to Santo Ovídio across a new Douro
bridge later still. A hydrogen **Metrobus** corridor began operating in 2026. (dates unverified —
they slip)

**Ticketing.** Everything runs on the **Andante** card. You **carregar o Andante** at a machine
or kiosk, then **validar** it at the yellow validator on the platform or as you board — a
sentence a resident says weekly: *Tenho de carregar o Andante* / *Já validaste?* Fares are by
zone (**Z2**, **Z3**…); as of 2026 a Z2 ticket is around €1.40 and the card itself around €0.60,
though prices change. There are monthly passes (**passe mensal**) and the **andante ocasional**
paper card for one-offs.

**Buses.** **STCP** runs inside the city of Porto and holds exclusivity there. Since 1 December
2023 the rest of the metropolitan area — Gaia, Matosinhos, Maia, Gondomar, Valongo, Vila do
Conde, Póvoa de Varzim, Santo Tirso, Trofa, Espinho and more — is served by the **UNIR** network,
around 439 lines, all on the same Andante ticketing.

**Trains.** Porto has two stations and they do different jobs. **Porto Campanhã** is the
long-distance station: **Alfa Pendular** and **Intercidades** to Lisbon and Braga, and the start
of most regional services. **Porto São Bento** is the beautiful city-centre terminus for
suburban and regional trains. From them run the **Linha do Douro** (Marco de Canaveses,
Peso da Régua, Pinhão, Tua, terminating at **Pocinho**, hugging the river east of Régua — the
scenic run every family does once a year), the **Linha do Minho** north through Nine and Barcelos
to Viana do Castelo and **Valença** on the Spanish border, and the **Linha de Guimarães**.

**Trams and funicular.** Three historic **elétricos** survive, run by STCP: **linha 1**
(Infante ↔ Passeio Alegre, along the river to Foz), **linha 18** (Carmo ↔ Passeio Alegre) and
**linha 22** (a circular between Carmo and Batalha) — though sources disagree on whether
linha 22 is currently running, so do not put a learner on it as though it certainly is.
The **Funicular dos Guindais** drops from Batalha to the Ribeira; it has been operated by
**STCP since 2022**, not by Metro do Porto.

**Driving.** The **A1** to Lisbon, **A3** to Braga and Valença, **A4** over the Marão to Vila
Real and Bragança, **A28** up the coast, **IP4** across Trás-os-Montes. **Portagens** and
**Via Verde** are constant conversation, as is the **VCI**, Porto's inner ring road, and its
morning **trânsito**.

**Markets and shopping.** The **Mercado do Bolhão** reopened on 15 September 2022 after a long
restoration — 81 **bancas**, 38 **lojas** and 10 restaurants, open Monday to Friday 08:00–20:00,
Saturday until 18:00, **closed Sundays**. The **Mercado do Bom Sucesso** near Boavista is the
modern food hall. Matosinhos has its **lota** and its fish. The **Feira de Barcelos** fills the
Campo da República **every Thursday** and has done since the seventeenth or eighteenth century —
vegetables and livestock alongside **louça de Barcelos**, the painted **galos**, baskets, linen
and ironwork. Bragança holds the **Feira das Cantarinhas** in the first days of May.

Everyday shops, with the words a resident uses: **o talho** (butcher), **a peixaria**
(fishmonger), **a padaria** and **a pastelaria**, **a frutaria**, **a mercearia** or
**o minimercado**, **a drogaria**, **a papelaria**, **a farmácia** with its green cross and its
rota of **farmácia de serviço** at night. Supermarkets: **Continente**, **Pingo Doce**, **Lidl**,
**Intermarché**, **Aldi**, **Minipreço**. Shopping centres: **NorteShopping** in Matosinhos,
**Via Catarina** on Rua de Santa Catarina, **Braga Parque**, **Espaço Guimarães**.

**Bureaucracy.** The **Junta de Freguesia** is the first stop for an **atestado de residência**
or to sort out **recenseamento eleitoral**. The **Câmara Municipal** handles licences and rates.
The **Loja do Cidadão** puts many services under one roof. **As Finanças** (the **Autoridade
Tributária**) issue the **NIF**, the **número de contribuinte**, which you are asked for at every
till — *Quer fatura com contribuinte?* — and where **IRS** is filed each spring on the
**Portal das Finanças**. The **Centro de Saúde** holds your **número de utente**; you
**marcar uma consulta** there, or ring **SNS 24** (unverified: 808 24 24 24). **Segurança
Social** and the **Segurança Social Direta** portal handle contributions and benefits. The
**CTT** post office is where you send a **carta registada** or **levantar uma encomenda**. The
**IMT** deals with the **carta de condução**. Residence permits for foreigners are now handled by
**AIMA** — the Agência para a Integração, Migrações e Asilo — which took over from the extinct
**SEF** when it was wound up in October 2023. The shared ritual of all of these: **tirar uma
senha**, wait for your number at **o balcão**, and complain about **a fila**.

**Money and bills.** **Multibanco** ATMs do far more than cash: **pagamento de serviços** lets
you pay **água**, **luz**, **gás**, taxes and fines with an **entidade**, a **referência** and a
**montante**. **MB WAY** handles phone-to-phone transfers and splitting a bill. Most households
put utilities on **débito direto**.

**Weekend and daily habits.** The morning **café** — *um cimbalino, se faz favor* — often with a
**pastel de nata** or **torrada**. Saturday morning at the market. Sunday lunch with the whole
family, running to three hours. A walk on the **Passeio Alegre** at Foz or along the Gaia
riverfront. The beach at **Matosinhos**, **Foz**, **Miramar**, **Espinho**, **Vila do Conde**, or
further north at **Afife** and **Moledo** — Atlantic, cold, windy, full of surfers. Football at
the weekend: **FC Porto** at the **Estádio do Dragão**, **Sporting Clube de Braga** — the
**Guerreiros do Minho**, the **Arsenalistas** — at the **Estádio Municipal de Braga**, known as
**a Pedreira**, Souto de Moura's quarry stadium opened in December 2003, **Vitória de Guimarães**
(**os Conquistadores**) at the **Estádio D. Afonso Henriques**, and **Boavista** at the **Bessa**.
In summer, the village **romaria**: a Mass, a procession, a **banda filarmónica**, grilled food,
a **palco** with a band until three in the morning, and **foguetes** fired at dawn.

## Festivals & calendar

**Fevereiro** — **Entrudo**/**Carnaval**. The **Caretos de Podence** (Macedo de Cavaleiros) run
through the village in fringed wool suits and brass masks (UNESCO intangible heritage, 2019 —
unverified). Bragança holds the **Festa do Butelo e das Casulas** in mid-February. **Feiras do
fumeiro** across Trás-os-Montes.

**Março/Abril** — **Semana Santa de Braga**: **Senhor dos Passos** on Palm Sunday, the
**Ecce Homo** with its **farricocos** on Maundy Thursday night, the **Enterro do Senhor** on Good
Friday, the **Ressurreição** at Easter. **Folar** is eaten at Easter and godchildren visit
godparents.

**Maio** — **Feira das Cantarinhas**, Bragança, the first days of May: three days of the biggest
traditional craft street fair in the region, named for the clay water jugs sold there. Barcelos
holds its **Festa das Cruzes** in early May (unverified date).

**Junho** — the **Santos Populares**, and above all **São João do Porto** on the night of 23 to
24 June, the biggest street party in northern Portugal. People hit each other on the head with
plastic **martelinhos** (a 1960s invention) or, in the older tradition, with **alho-porro**
(leek); carry a **manjerico** with a rhyming **quadra** stuck in it; grill **sardinhas** in the
street; build **cascatas** (miniature scenes of São João); launch paper **balões**; watch the
fireworks over the Douro at midnight; and walk down to Foz to see the sun come up. 24 June is
Porto's **feriado municipal**. Braga keeps its own **São João** (unverified detail). Amarante's
**Festas de São Gonçalo** fall in June (unverified date).

**Agosto** — **Festas Gualterianas**, Guimarães, always the first weekend of August, in honour of
São Gualter: the **Cortejo do Linho**, the **Batalha das Flores** and the closing **Marcha
Gualteriana**. **Romaria de Nossa Senhora da Agonia**, Viana do Castelo, around 20 August (the
town's **feriado municipal**), running since 1783 — the **mordomas** parade in **traje à vianesa**
laden with gold **filigrana**, the streets are carpeted with coloured sawdust and flowers, and
the image goes down to the sea. The whole month is also when the **emigrantes** come home.

**Setembro** — the **vindima**. In the Douro the grapes are picked off the **socalcos** and, at
many quintas, still trodden by foot in granite **lagares** — the **pisa a pé** — and in the Minho
the **vinho verde** harvest runs at the same time. **Feiras Novas**, Ponte de Lima, in September:
the romaria of Nossa Senhora das Dores, with the **Vaca das Cordas**, ethnographic parades,
philharmonic bands, concertina competitions and fireworks.

**Novembro** — **São Martinho**, 11 November: the **magusto**, roasted **castanhas**, **jeropiga**
and **água-pé**. **As Nicolinas** in Guimarães, 29 November to 7 December, the students' festival
for São Nicolau, which the whole city joins.

**Dezembro** — **Consoada** on the 24th. In some Trás-os-Montes villages the **Festa dos Rapazes**
and its own masked **caretos** run between Christmas and Epiphany (unverified).

## Language notes

The northern accent is the most recognisable in Portugal, and northerners are proud of it.

**Betacismo — the b/v merger.** In much of the north, especially the Minho and rural Braga,
/v/ is realised as [b]: *vaca* sounds like *baca*, *vinho* like *binho*, *vem* like *bem*. It is
the single most commented-on feature of northern speech, mocked in Lisbon and worn as a badge
locally. It is strongest in older and rural speakers and less consistent in urban Porto.

**Diphthongs are preserved.** Where Lisbon monophthongises, the north keeps the glide:

- **ou** stays as [ow] — *pouco*, *outro*, *sou*, *roupa* — while standard Lisbon pronunciation
  flattens it to [o].
- **ei** stays as [ej] — *dinheiro*, *primeiro*, *deixei*, *eira* — where the south reduces it to
  [e] and Lisbon shifts it towards [ɐj].

**Vowels stay fuller.** Northern speakers do not crush unstressed vowels the way Lisbon does:
*Filipa* is said with all its syllables, not squeezed to *Flipa*. The overall effect is slower,
rounder and more open, where Lisbon sounds clipped and consonantal.

**The apical s.** Along the northern coast and across the border in Galicia, /s/ is articulated
with the tip of the tongue, producing a whistly, slightly *sh*-tinted sibilant that southern ears
notice immediately.

**Braga's cadence.** Braga's accent is more strongly marked and more sung than Porto's, with a
rising melody. Typical Braga realisations: *meu* → *mieu*, *bem* → *beim*.

**Conservative features inland.** In parts of the Minho and Trás-os-Montes the affricate **ch**
[tʃ] is still kept distinct from **x** [ʃ] — *chave* does not rhyme with *xaile* — and the
second-person plural **vós** forms (*vós ides*, *vós fazeis*) survive in older rural speech.
(both unverified)

**Mirandês.** Portugal's second official language, recognised by Lei 7/99 of 29 January 1999. It
is Asturian-Leonese, not a dialect of Portuguese, spoken across roughly 500 km² in almost all the
villages of the concelho of **Miranda do Douro** and in three villages of **Vimioso** (Vilar
Seco, Angueira, Caçarelhos), on the Zamora border. The **Pauliteiros de Miranda** dance the stick
dance to **gaita-de-foles**, **caixa** and **bombo**.

**Register.** *Tu* with family, friends and neighbours. *O senhor* / *a senhora* with older
people, shopkeepers you don't know and anyone behind a counter. Third-person address by name is
normal and polite: *A Dona Rosa quer um café?* Northerners are warm and blunt at the same time;
diminutives are everywhere — *um cafezinho*, *uma sopinha*, *um bocadinho*.

**Local words** (each `palavra` — meaning):

- `cimbalino` — an espresso, in Porto; elsewhere `bica` or just `café`
- `fino` — a small draught beer; `imperial` in Lisbon
- `molete` — a wheat bread roll
- `chuço` — an umbrella
- `briol` — biting cold
- `sertã` — a frying pan; `frigideira` elsewhere
- `cruzeta` — a coat hanger; `cabide` elsewhere
- `safa` — a rubber/eraser; `borracha` elsewhere
- `aguça` — a pencil sharpener; `afia` elsewhere
- `chicla` — chewing gum; `pastilha elástica` elsewhere
- `estrugido` — the onion-and-garlic base fried in olive oil; `refogado` elsewhere
- `magnório` — a medlar; `nêspera` elsewhere
- `sapatilhas` — trainers; `ténis` elsewhere
- `repas` — a fringe of hair; `franja` elsewhere
- `mouco` — deaf, hard of hearing
- `morcão` — a slow, oafish person
- `carago` — an all-purpose Porto exclamation
- `à minha beira` — beside me, next to me
- `volta` — a gold or silver chain worn round the neck
- `tripeiro` — a native of Porto
- `Invicta` — Porto's epithet, "the unconquered"
- `socalco` — a vineyard terrace on a Douro hillside
- `quinta` — a wine estate, or any country property
- `lagar` — the granite trough where grapes are trodden
- `espigueiro` — a raised stone granary on stilts

## Towns

### Porto

The working capital of the north, built in granite on both banks above the Douro mouth. Known
for the Ribeira, the Torre dos Clérigos, São Bento's tiles, the Ponte Luís I and the football.
Landmarks: **Torre dos Clérigos** and **Estação de São Bento**. Food: the **francesinha**, and
**tripas à moda do Porto** on a Sunday. Everyday life: the metro converges at **Trindade**, the
**Mercado do Bolhão** is closed on Sundays so shopping is a Saturday-morning job, and people say
*um cimbalino* when they mean an espresso.

### Vila Nova de Gaia

Across the river from Porto and a bigger city than most people realise. Known for the port wine
**caves** — Sandeman, Ferreira and dozens more — lining the **Cais de Gaia**, with **barcos
rabelo** moored in front. Landmarks: the **Mosteiro da Serra do Pilar** and the **teleférico**.
Food: port wine itself, tasted in the lodges; the Gaia coast eats grilled fish. Everyday life:
metro line D crosses the top deck of the Ponte Luís I and runs down through Santo Ovídio to
**Vila d'Este**, so commuting into Porto is a metro journey, not a drive.

### Braga

The religious capital — an archbishopric since Roman times, and Portugal's oldest city as
**Bracara Augusta**, founded 16 BC. Also, thanks to the Universidade do Minho, one of its
youngest. Landmarks: the **Sé**, consecrated 1089, and **Bom Jesus do Monte** with its 581-step
escadório and 1882 water-powered funicular. Food: **bacalhau à Braga** (à Narcisa), postas fried
in olive oil over sliced potato under a blanket of onion. Everyday life: **Semana Santa** empties
the offices and fills the streets; the rest of the year people meet under **a Arcada** on the
Avenida da Liberdade.

### Guimarães

The birthplace of the country — *Aqui nasceu Portugal* is carved on the old wall. UNESCO historic
centre, European Capital of Culture 2012. Landmarks: the tenth-century **Castelo** and the
**Paço dos Duques de Bragança** (1420–1433). Food: doces conventuais and the food stalls of the
**Festas Gualterianas** on the first weekend of August. Everyday life: the student festival of
**As Nicolinas**, 29 November to 7 December, takes over the city; and on match days **Vitória**
fills the Estádio D. Afonso Henriques.

### Viana do Castelo

Where the Lima meets the Atlantic — a shipbuilding town with the most photogenic traditional
costume in Portugal. Landmarks: the **Santuário de Santa Luzia** on the hill, reached by
funicular, and the hospital ship **Gil Eannes** in the harbour. Food: **bolas de Berlim** from
Confeitaria Manuel Natário, and fish off the Atlantic. Everyday life: the **estaleiros navais**
are still a major employer, the goldsmiths still make **filigrana**, and the whole town takes 20
August off for the **Romaria de Nossa Senhora da Agonia**.

### Barcelos

A Minho market town on the Cávado and the source of the national symbol. Known for the legend and
the pottery of the **galo de Barcelos**, and for **figurado** — painted clay figures. Landmarks:
the **Torre da Porta Nova** and the ruined **Paço dos Condes**. Food: the fair's own rural
produce — **broa**, **vinho verde**, smoked meats. Everyday life: the whole town revolves around
**Thursday**, when the **Feira de Barcelos** fills the Campo da República with vegetables,
livestock, pottery, baskets and linen. Pilgrims on the **Caminho Português** pass through
constantly.

### Amarante

A Tâmega town at the foot of the Serra do Marão, built around its bridge. Landmarks: the
**Ponte de São Gonçalo** and the church and convent of São Gonçalo beside it; the **Museu Amadeo
de Souza-Cardoso**. Food: the **doces conventuais** — **foguetes**, **lérias**, **brisas do
Tâmega**, **papos de anjo** — and the notorious phallic **bolos de São Gonçalo**, sold openly
again since 1974. Everyday life: Confeitaria da Ponte has been the meeting point since 1930, and
single women are still teased about São Gonçalo, the matchmaking saint.

### Bragança

The far north-east, up against the Spanish border, high and cold. Known for its walled
**Cidadela**, its isolation, and the surrounding **Parque Natural de Montesinho**. Landmarks: the
**Castelo** keep and the twelfth-century **Domus Municipalis**, unique in Portugal. Food:
**butelo com casulas** (celebrated at a February festival), **posta mirandesa**, **alheira**,
**folar** and the whole world of **fumeiro**. Everyday life: winter is long — *nove meses de
inverno e três de inferno* — the **Feira das Cantarinhas** opens May, and Mirandês is still
spoken in the villages towards Miranda do Douro.

### Peso da Régua e Pinhão

The heart of the **Alto Douro Vinhateiro**, UNESCO World Heritage since 2001, in a wine region
demarcated in 1756. Known for the terraced **socalcos** and the **quintas** with their names
painted on the hillsides. Landmarks: Pinhão's azulejo-tiled railway station and the river itself.
Food and drink: port wine and Douro table wine; heavy, hot-weather cooking. Everyday life: the
**Linha do Douro** train from Porto is the artery — it runs beside the water east of Régua — and
in **September** everything stops for the **vindima**, with grapes still trodden by foot in
granite **lagares**.

## Prompt context

Learners here live around Porto, Vila Nova de Gaia, Braga, Guimarães, Viana do Castelo, the
Minho villages, the Douro valley or Trás-os-Montes. Reach for the metro do Porto (linhas A–F,
interchange at Trindade, carregar o Andante, validar), STCP buses inside Porto and UNIR beyond
it, comboios from Campanhã and São Bento along the Linha do Douro to Régua and Pinhão or the
Linha do Minho to Viana and Valença, the elétrico 1 to Foz. Errands: Mercado do Bolhão (closed
Sundays), the Thursday feira de Barcelos, o talho, a peixaria, a padaria, a mercearia, Continente
and Pingo Doce, a farmácia, a Junta de Freguesia for an atestado de residência, as Finanças and
the NIF, o Centro de Saúde and the número de utente, os CTT, pagamento de serviços at the
Multibanco, MB WAY. Foods: francesinha, tripas à moda do Porto, bacalhau à Gomes de Sá, rojões,
papas de sarrabulho, caldo verde, broa, alheira de Mirandela, posta mirandesa, sardinhas assadas
at São João, vinho verde, vinho do Porto, um fino, um cimbalino. Habits: rain and guarda-chuvas,
Sunday lunch, Matosinhos beach, FC Porto at o Dragão, romarias with bandas filarmónicas, the
vindima in September, magusto at São Martinho.
