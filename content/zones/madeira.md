# Zona: Madeira

## Identity

Madeira is an autonomous region (Região Autónoma da Madeira), not a district — people say **a Região**, and they say **o continente** for mainland Portugal. Going to Lisbon is *ir ao continente*, and it involves an airport, not a motorway. The archipelago is Madeira, Porto Santo, and the uninhabited Desertas and Selvagens. Roughly a quarter of a million people live here, about 105,795 of them in Funchal alone (2021 census), which means almost everyone either lives in Funchal, works in Funchal, or drives through it.

The island is vertical. Nothing is far in kilometres and everything is far in altitude. A resident's sense of place is built on *subir* and *descer* — you go **up** to Monte, **down** to the marina, and the bus fare and the weather both change on the way. Neighbours are close because the terrain forces them to be: houses stack on the hillside, and the *quinta* above you can see into your yard.

The other durable identity marker is emigration. Madeirans left for Venezuela, South Africa, the Channel Islands, the UK. Almost every family has someone *lá fora*, and Portuguese here is spoken with the awareness that a cousin will phone from Caracas at an inconvenient hour. Returned emigrants and their Spanish- or English-inflected Portuguese are ordinary, not exotic.

Madeirans are proud, funny, and quietly competitive with the Azores about which archipelago is the real Atlantic Portugal. They are extremely proud of Cristiano Ronaldo, whose name is on the airport.

## Geography & climate

Madeira is a volcanic ridge dropped into the Atlantic, and the ridge does the weather. **Pico Ruivo** (1,862 m) and **Pico do Arieiro** (1,818 m) sit in the middle; the pass at **Encumeada** is where the island's two halves argue. The **south coast** (Funchal, Câmara de Lobos, Caniço) is dry, warm and sheltered. The **north coast** (São Vicente, Santana, Porto Moniz) is green, wet and dramatic. It is entirely normal to leave Funchal in sunshine, come out of the tunnel at São Vicente in rain, and drive back into sunshine an hour later. Residents plan around this rather than complain about it.

The interior holds the **Laurissilva**, the ancient laurel forest, a UNESCO World Heritage site and the reason the island holds water at all.

It is rarely cold. Funchal winters sit around comfortable jumper weather; summers are warm without being brutal, and the sea is swimmable most of the year. What varies is cloud and wind, not temperature. Funchal frequently wears a flat lid of cloud sitting on the hills above the city, known locally as the **capacete** — the helmet (unverified as a formal term, but universally understood). A **pé de vento** is a sudden violent squall that arrives from nowhere, tears the washing off the line and is gone in ten minutes.

Wind is the single most consequential fact of Madeiran life, because of the airport. **Aeroporto Internacional da Madeira — Cristiano Ronaldo**, at Santa Cruz, has a runway extended out over the sea on **180 pillars**, some 60 m tall — an engineering job that won the 2004 IABSE Outstanding Structure Award. It is widely described as one of the most demanding approaches in the world; pilots need a **special licence** to operate there, and crosswind gusts routinely cause go-arounds and diversions. "O avião foi desviado" is a normal sentence here.

**Porto Santo**, 40-odd km northeast, is the opposite island: flat, dry, semi-arid, with a single golden beach nine kilometres long and about 5,149 residents (2021). Madeirans go there to be flat for a weekend.

## Landmarks & buildings

- **Sé Catedral do Funchal** — begun under Pêro Anes, completed 1514, with a *teto de alfarge* in island wood that locals will tell you is one of the finest ceilings in Portugal.
- **Mercado dos Lavradores** — the 1940s market in Funchal. Flower sellers at the entrance, fruit sellers upstairs, and the fish hall downstairs where *espada preta* is laid out in long black rows.
- **Zona Velha** and **Rua de Santa Maria** — the old town, where the doors were painted by artists as part of the *Projeto Arte de Portas Abertas*.
- **Fortaleza de São Tiago** and **Fortaleza-Palácio de São Lourenço** — the two forts on the Funchal waterfront.
- **Monte** — reached by the **Teleférico do Monte** cable car; the **carros de cesto**, wicker sledges on a wooden *corsa*, are pushed downhill by two **carreiros** in white with straw hats. They began in the mid-19th century as actual transport from Monte down to the city; today the run stops at Livramento.
- **Jardim Botânico da Madeira** and the Monte gardens.
- **Cabo Girão** — one of Europe's highest sea cliffs, with a glass-floored *skywalk*.
- **Curral das Freiras** — a village in a bowl of mountains in the Câmara de Lobos municipality, famous for chestnuts.
- **Ponta de São Lourenço** — the bare, wind-scoured eastern tip; the walk everyone does when it is too windy to do anything else.
- **Casas típicas de Santana** — triangular thatched houses, the island's postcard.
- **Piscinas naturais do Porto Moniz** — volcanic rock pools filled by the sea.
- **Via Rápida (VR1)** and the tunnels — the road network that turned a two-hour journey into twenty minutes. Madeirans measure distance in tunnels.
- **Teatro Municipal Baltazar Dias** on Avenida Arriaga, and the **Marina do Funchal**.

## Churches & religious sites

The **Diocese do Funchal** is one of the oldest Portuguese dioceses. Beyond the **Sé**:

- **Igreja e Convento de Santa Clara** (1492–1497), in a Hispano-Arab style, holding the tomb of João Gonçalves Zarco.
- **Igreja do Colégio** — the Jesuit church on Praça do Município, tiled floor to ceiling.
- **Nossa Senhora do Monte** — the pilgrimage church above Funchal, reached by a long flight of steps that some pilgrims climb on their knees on 15 August, and the resting place of the last Austro-Hungarian emperor, Carlos I, who died in exile in Madeira. The 15 August *romaria* is the island's biggest religious day; it is also remembered for the day in 2017 when a centuries-old oak came down on the crowd at the Largo da Fonte and killed thirteen people, which is why the trees there are now watched closely.
- **Capela dos Milagres**, Machico — the Chapel of Miracles, built in the mid-15th century, rebuilt in the 16th and again in 1883, damaged by the 1956 flood and restored in 1957. Its festival on 8/9 October is Machico's biggest day.
- **Igreja de São Bento**, Ribeira Brava, and the parish churches of Santana, Porto Moniz and Calheta.

Practically, religion shows up as **arraiais** — the parish festival with a stage, a band, a *bailinho*, grilled meat and beer on a Saturday night — and as **romarias**, the walking pilgrimages. The *arraial* is where the Portuguese a learner needs is actually spoken.

## Food & drink

**Espetada em pau de louro** — beef cut *em cubos*, salted heavily, garlic and bay, threaded onto a **laurel-wood skewer** and grilled over coals. It comes from **Estreito de Câmara de Lobos**. Restaurants increasingly use iron or aluminium skewers; whether that counts is an argument you can start at any table. Served hanging from a stand over the plate, with **milho frito**, salad and bread.

**Bolo do caco** — a flat, soft, round bread made with **batata-doce** (sweet potato, added historically to stretch scarce flour), cooked on a hot **caco** — originally a broken roof tile, now usually a metal plate. Eaten hot, split, and drowned in **manteiga de alho** with parsley. Also split and filled as a *prego no bolo do caco*.

**Milho frito** — cubes of set cornmeal, fried until crisp outside and soft inside. Non-negotiable with espetada.

**Lapas grelhadas** — limpets grilled in their shells with garlic butter and a wedge of lemon squeezed over the lot at the table.

**Espada com banana** — fillets of black scabbardfish, fried, with fried banana. The combination sounds odd to visitors and is completely ordinary to residents.

**Carne de vinha d'alhos** — pork marinated in wine vinegar, garlic and bay for days, then fried. This is the Christmas dish. If someone's kitchen smells of vinegar in mid-December, that is why.

Also everyday: **picado** (fried beef pieces with garlic and chips, eaten communally with cocktail sticks, usually late and usually with beer), **sopa de trigo**, **caldeirada**, **atum com milho**, **gaiado**.

Sweet: **bolo de mel de cana** — the dark spiced honey cake made in November for Christmas, which you *break with your hands*, never cut with a knife; **broas de mel**; **queijadas**; **rebuçados de funcho** (fennel sweets); **malassadas** and **sonhos** at Carnaval.

Fruit: **banana da Madeira** (small, sweet, grown on terraced **poios**), **anona** (cherimoya, winter, and Faial has a *Festa da Anona*), **maracujá** in several varieties, **tomate inglês**.

**Poncha** — the drink. *Aguardente de cana*, sugar or **mel de cana**, and lemon juice, worked together with a wooden stirrer called a **caralhinho** (also, more politely, *mexelote* or *pau de mexer*). Protected as **Poncha da Madeira** by regional legislation in 2014, made only in Madeira and Porto Santo. Variants: **poncha regional** (lemon), **poncha pescador** (the fisherman's, stronger, from Câmara de Lobos), **poncha de maracujá** or *de tangerina*. Its ancestry runs back to 16th-century sailors preserving lemon in spirit against scurvy — the same lineage that produced the caipirinha.

**Nikita** — beer, pineapple ice cream and white wine blended together, born in Câmara de Lobos. Ordered by the jug, shared, drunk standing.

**Vinho da Madeira** — fortified, effectively immortal. Four noble varieties by sweetness: **Sercial** (dry), **Verdelho** (medium-dry), **Boal** (medium-sweet), **Malvasia** (sweet); the other ~90% of production is **Tinta Negra**. Aged either by **estufagem** — held near 50 °C for about ninety days, then ninety more at ambient — or, for the good stuff, by **canteiro**, natural ageing in cask in the roof of the lodge. Houses include Madeira Wine Company, Henriques & Henriques, Pereira d'Oliveira, Vinhos Barbeito, Justino's, H. M. Borges, Artur de Barros e Sousa and Madeira Vintners. Locally it is drunk as *aperitivo* or *digestivo*, served at 13–16 °C.

Local soft drinks and beer: **Coral** (brewed on the island), **Brisa** (maracujá and laranja), and **Laranjada**, the historic orange soda.

## Restaurants & institutions

**Mercado dos Lavradores, Funchal.** Saturday morning is the ritual. You come early, you get pounced on by a fruit seller offering you a slice of *maracujá-banana* on the tip of a knife, and there is a well-understood dance in which you taste, admire, and negotiate. Downstairs in the fish hall the *espada preta* lie in black ranks with their teeth showing, and someone will fillet one in about nine seconds while telling you what to do with it. The flower sellers at the entrance wear the traditional *traje* with the little **carapuça** hat.

**Noite do Mercado.** On the night of 23 December the market and the streets around it stay open all night and the whole of Funchal turns up (unverified: exact date varies in reporting). People buy the Christmas fruit and meat at two in the morning, drink poncha, and sing *Missa do Parto* songs badly. Nobody sleeps and nobody minds.

**Espetada houses of Estreito de Câmara de Lobos and Santo António** — the traditional espetada is a Sunday-lunch institution up in the hills above Câmara de Lobos. **As Vides**, on Rua da Achada in Estreito, has been doing it since **1950** and is generally described as the oldest espetada house on the island; it is the name most locals give when you ask where to go, and it is still trading. The ritual: you order by the number of skewers, not by person; the bread and *milho frito* land before you have decided anything; and there is always one person at the table who insists the laurel wood makes the difference and one who insists it does not.

**Câmara de Lobos poncha bars.** Small, tiled, loud, standing room. You order at the counter, the *poncheiro* works the *caralhinho* in a fast wrist-flick that everyone watches and nobody can reproduce, and the glass arrives without ice. The nikita comes in a jug for the table.

**Blandy's Wine Lodge**, Funchal — the Madeira Wine Company's lodge in the centre of town, with more than 650 casks and vats ageing *ao canteiro*, a shop, and tours and tastings daily.

**Fábrica Santo António**, Travessa do Forno — the Funchal confectioner founded in **1893**, the island's first fine-biscuit works, where the *bolo de mel de cana*, the *broas* and the *rebuçados de funcho* are still made. Open Monday to Friday and Saturday mornings; shut on Sundays.

**Teleférico do Monte / Carreiros do Monte** — the *carros de cesto* are worked by the **carreiros**, in white cotton and straw hats, with rubber-soled boots for braking.

**Santo da Serra market**, Sunday mornings, and **Camacha** for wickerwork — the Sunday drive with a purpose.

**The Ritz Madeira** at Avenida Arriaga 33 — one of the oldest of the grand Funchal cafés, over a century in business, open every day, its tiled façade and terrace facing the municipal gardens. This and the other Avenida Arriaga cafés are where the *bica* and the *pastel de nata* at eleven happen: the newspaper, the same table, the same waiter.

Affectionate observations worth reusing in dialogue: the eternal debate over how much *mel de cana* belongs in a poncha and whether stirring it hard "kills" it; the seriousness with which someone breaks the *bolo de mel* with their hands and the scandal if you reach for a knife; the fact that everyone claims their grandmother's *vinha d'alhos* needed exactly one more day.

## Everyday life & errands

Buses in the city are **Horários do Funchal** (the yellow ones); out of the city you take **SAM** to the east, **Rodoeste** to the west, and **EACL** around Câmara de Lobos (unverified for current operator names). Parking in Funchal is a sport; residents talk about *o parque* and *a zona azul* the way mainlanders talk about the weather.

Because of the tunnels, the island is small in time and huge in feeling. Getting to Porto Moniz used to be a day; now it is an afternoon.

**Levadas** are the island's plumbing and its walking network at once: roughly 800 km of main channels and 2,300 km of secondary ones, cut into rock from the 15th century onward to move water from the wet north to the dry, cultivated south. They fed mills, then sugar cane, then bananas. A **levadeiro** manages the flow and the water rights, and *turnos de água* — whose turn it is to irrigate — are a real, negotiated, occasionally heated part of rural life. The maintenance paths beside them are how everyone walks: **Levada das 25 Fontes**, **Levada do Caldeirão Verde**, **Levada dos Balcões**, **Levada do Rei**. A **vereda** is the steeper footpath. The system was submitted for UNESCO consideration in 2017.

Farming is terraced. A **poio** is one terrace; bananas, vines and *semilhas* grow on them, and the family plot above the house is still normal.

Fishing out of **Câmara de Lobos** means **espada preta**, caught deep at night on long lines — in 2011 it accounted for about 43% of all fish landed in the region. Câmara de Lobos also supplies about 62% of the grapes that become Madeira wine.

Shopping: **Fórum Madeira**, **MadeiraShopping**, **La Vie** and **Anadia** in and around Funchal; Pingo Doce and Continente for groceries.

Getting off the island: flights from Santa Cruz to Lisbon and Porto, and **Binter** to Porto Santo in about fifteen minutes. By sea it is the **Lobo Marinho** ferry from Funchal to Porto Santo — a couple of hours, and *how* those hours go depends entirely on the swell. Everybody has an opinion on which side of the boat to sit.

Institutions: **Hospital Dr. Nélio Mendonça** in Funchal, the **Universidade da Madeira**, the **Governo Regional** and the **Assembleia Legislativa**.

## Festivals & calendar

- **15 January — Santo Amaro.** The day the Christmas decorations come down, celebrated with a *varrer dos armários* and sweets; an unmistakably Madeiran habit of ending Christmas properly rather than letting it fade.
- **February/March — Carnaval do Funchal.** A full allegorical parade plus the *Cortejo Trapalhão*, the deliberately ridiculous one. **Malassadas** and **sonhos** everywhere.
- **March/April — Páscoa.** *Carne de vinha d'alhos* returns.
- **April/May — Festa da Flor.** Held since 1954 (originally the *Festa da Rosa*), four days of *cortejos*, flower exhibitions and **tapetes florais**. Children lay flowers on the **Muro da Esperança** at Largo do Município on the Saturday; the big **cortejo alegórico** with flower-covered floats runs on the Sunday.
- **June — Festival do Atlântico**, Saturday-night fireworks over the bay through the month.
- **1 July — Dia da Região Autónoma da Madeira**, regional holiday.
- **Summer — arraiais** in every parish, plus the **Semana do Mar**, the **Festa do Senhor Bom Jesus** and Machico's **Festa do Santíssimo Sacramento**.
- **August — Festa da Nossa Senhora do Monte** on the 15th, the island's biggest religious day.
- **Late August/September — Festa do Vinho Madeira** in Funchal and Estreito de Câmara de Lobos, with the *vindima* and grape-treading.
- **August — Rali Vinho da Madeira**, when the whole island stands on a corner.
- **October — Festa dos Milagres**, Machico (8/9 October); **Festa da Anona** in Faial.
- **November — Festa da Castanha**, Curral das Freiras.
- **December — Festas de Fim do Ano.** From 8 December the Funchal lights go on. The **Missas do Parto** are the nine dawn masses before Christmas, each followed by a *festa* outside the church with *poncha*, *carne de vinha d'alhos* and *bailinho*. The **Noite do Mercado** on 23 December. Then the 31 December fireworks over Funchal bay, which the whole island watches from a hillside and which have held a Guinness record.

## Language notes

Madeiran Portuguese is European Portuguese — everything a learner needs (tu register, *pequeno-almoço*, *estar a + infinitivo*) holds. What changes is the sound and a layer of local vocabulary. Sandra should teach standard European Portuguese and *describe* these features so the learner recognises them in the street.

**Phonology — the reliable markers:**

- `família` → *famílhia* — /l/ palatalises after /i/. Same in `vila` → *vilha*, `aquilo` → *aquilho*, `Filipe` → *Filhipe*. This one is shared with the **Açores**, and it is the quickest reliable way to place a speaker as an islander.
- `fazer` → *fazêre / fazêri* — the final -e of infinitives is kept and pronounced, where Lisbon drops it. Also `comer` → *comêre*, `falar` → *falare*. **This is the single most caricatured island trait on the mainland**; a learner producing it in Lisbon would be heard as doing an impression.
- `olho` → *uôlho* — a /u/ glide slipped in before a stressed vowel. Also `formiga` → *formúiga*, `livro` → *lúivro*. Instantly recognisable, and the one visitors notice first.
- `osso` → *osse* — final -o closing to -e. Also `macaco` → *macaque*, `carro` → *carre*.
- `paragem` → *parage* — -agem reduced to -age. Also `viagem` → *viage*.
- `ficaram` → *ficáru* — third-person plural preterite -am reduced. Also `foram` → *fôru*.
- `os` / `as` → *ui* / *ai* in rapid speech.

**Grammar:** Madeiran speech shows a marked preference for the **gerúndio** (*estou fazendo*) where Lisbon uses *estar a + infinitivo* (*estou a fazer*), and for **a gente** over *nós*. This is the one that carries a trap, because *estou fazendo* is **also** the standard Brazilian form. It is not a Brazilianism when a Madeiran says it — it is an independent southern European Portuguese feature with its own history, and it must never be corrected in a local speaker. But the learner should produce *estar a + infinitivo* and *nós*, because in a foreign mouth the gerund will be heard as Brazilian.

Everything above is **recognition, not production**. The learner's target is one neutral European Portuguese; copying these features reads as mimicry, while using the local *vocabulary* below reads as belonging. The cross-zone atlas in `sotaques.md` carries the full picture.

**Lexicon:**

- `semilha` — potato (mainland *batata*). The most famous Madeiran word.
- `poio` — a cultivated terrace cut into the hillside.
- `levada` — the irrigation channel; also, by extension, the walk beside it.
- `levadeiro` — the man who manages a levada and its water turns.
- `vereda` — a steep footpath, usually up.
- `caco` — the hot stone or tile that *bolo do caco* is baked on.
- `corsa` — the wooden sledge under a *carro de cesto*.
- `carreiro` — the man who pushes and brakes it; also a narrow path.
- `achada` — a flat shelf of land high up (Achada do Teixeira).
- `lombo` — a ridge running down a hillside; all over the map.
- `fajã` — flat land at the foot of a cliff, made by landslip or lava.
- `pé de vento` — a sudden violent squall.
- `capacete` — the flat cloud lid over Funchal (unverified as a formal term; universally understood).
- `espada` — the black scabbardfish, *peixe-espada-preto*, not a sword.
- `caralhinho` — the wooden stick used to work poncha; *mexelote* is the polite alternative.
- `mel de cana` — sugar-cane molasses, not honey. *Mel de abelha* is honey.
- `bila` — town, the centre, Funchal (unverified).
- `furado` — a tunnel cut through rock (Levada do Furado) (unverified in everyday use).
- `o continente` — mainland Portugal. *Ir ao continente* = to fly to Lisbon.
- `lá fora` — abroad, where the emigrant relatives are.

## Towns

### Funchal

Known for: the capital, and roughly 105,795 of the region's people (2021), spread over ten *freguesias* climbing the amphitheatre above the bay. Landmark: the **Sé Catedral** with its island-timber ceiling, and the **Mercado dos Lavradores** a few streets down. Food detail: *bolo do caco* with garlic butter sold from a hatch in the Zona Velha at midnight, and *pastel de nata* with a *bica* on Avenida Arriaga at eleven in the morning. Everyday life: Horários do Funchal buses, the parking hunt, the tunnels out, and the fact that the whole city can see whether the *capacete* has come down over the hills before deciding what to do with Saturday.

### Câmara de Lobos

Known for: fishing — the **espada preta** fleet, and about 43% of the region's landed catch (2011) — and for wine, supplying about 62% of the grapes for Madeira wine. Landmark: the working harbour full of painted boats, and **Cabo Girão** above it. Food detail: this is the birthplace of **poncha pescador** and of **nikita**; the espetada tradition belongs to the parish of **Estreito de Câmara de Lobos** just uphill. Everyday life: five parishes, from the harbour up to **Curral das Freiras** in its ring of mountains and **Jardim da Serra** with the cherry blossom in spring; men mend gear on the seafront in the afternoon and the boats go out at dusk.

### Machico

Known for: where Madeira began — Zarco and Tristão Vaz Teixeira came ashore here between 1418 and 1420. Landmark: the **Capela dos Milagres**, mid-15th century, rebuilt after the 1956 flood. Food detail: the seafront cafés and the *Festa dos Milagres* food stalls in October. Everyday life: about 19,617 residents (2021) across five parishes; the airport is next door at Santa Cruz, so half the town hears the go-arounds; the town beach is imported golden sand, which the *praias de areia negra* elsewhere in the municipality — Caniçal, Porto da Cruz — make people notice.

### Santana

Known for: the **casas típicas** — triangular, thatched, painted red, white and blue — and UNESCO **Reserva Mundial da Biosfera** status. Landmark: the row of restored *casas* by the town hall, and the trailheads up to **Pico Ruivo** from Achada do Teixeira. Food detail: the north-coast farming produce, and *licor* and cake at the *quintas*. Everyday life: the coolest, wettest inhabited part of the island — summers averaging about 17 °C, winters about 10.5 °C, and around 2,000 mm of rain a year, with fog a normal condition rather than an event. People here own proper coats.

### Porto Santo

Known for: nine kilometres of beach and a semi-arid climate that has nothing in common with Madeira's. Landmark: **Vila Baleira**, the *Casa Colombo* museum where Christopher Columbus is said to have lived after marrying the daughter of the first *donatário*, Bartolomeu Perestrelo. Food detail: the beach *quiosques*, and the bolo do caco that gets eaten with sand on it. Everyday life: about 5,149 residents (2021); the **Lobo Marinho** ferry from Funchal or a fifteen-minute Binter flight; the sand has a long local reputation as therapeutic (*areoterapia*), the golf course was designed by Severiano Ballesteros, and the island became a UNESCO biosphere reserve in October 2020. In winter it is very quiet and everyone knows everyone.

### Porto Moniz

Known for: the **piscinas naturais**, volcanic rock pools that the sea refills. Landmark: the pools themselves and the whale museum. Food detail: north-coast *lapas* and fresh fish, eaten with the Atlantic exploding twenty metres away. Everyday life: the far northwest corner; the old cliff road was replaced by tunnels, which locals over fifty will tell you about at length.

### Ribeira Brava

Known for: being the natural stop between Funchal and the west, at the mouth of a valley. Landmark: **Igreja de São Bento**, with its 16th-century font and tiled tower. Food detail: the seafront restaurants and the pebble beach *esplanadas*. Everyday life: the market, the school run down the valley, and the road up to **Encumeada** where the weather changes on you.

### São Vicente

Known for: the **Grutas de São Vicente**, lava tubes you can walk through, and the wettest, greenest side of the island. Landmark: the small chapel built into a boulder at the river mouth. Food detail: *sopa de trigo* and north-coast cooking in the village restaurants. Everyday life: the tunnel to Seixal and Porto Moniz; when the *ribeira* is high after rain, everyone goes to look at it.

### Caniço e Santa Cruz (Madeira)

East of Funchal either side of the airport: Caniço de Baixo has the island's largest resident foreign community, and Santa Cruz is the municipality the airport sits in. Not to be confused with Santa Cruz in the Oeste.

### Calheta

On the sunnier south-west coast, with one of the island's few imported-sand beaches and the Casa das Mudas arts centre.

### Ponta do Sol

A small south-coast town that markets itself as a **digital nomad village**, with the island's highest sunshine hours.

## Prompt context

The learner lives in Madeira, an autonomous Atlantic region where mainland Portugal is called *o continente* and going there means flying. The island is vertical: you go up to Monte and down to the marina, and journeys are measured in tunnels on the Via Rápida. Funchal holds most of the population; Câmara de Lobos fishes *espada preta* and grows the wine grapes; Santana is cold and wet; Porto Santo is flat and dry, two hours away on the *Lobo Marinho*. Weather dictates plans — the cloud lid over Funchal, a sudden *pé de vento*, crosswinds diverting flights at the pillared runway in Santa Cruz. Everyday nouns: *levada*, *levadeiro*, *poio*, *vereda*, *semilha*, *arraial*, *bica*. Everyday food: *espetada* on a laurel skewer with *milho frito*, *bolo do caco* with *manteiga de alho*, *lapas*, *espada com banana*, *carne de vinha d'alhos* at Christmas, *bolo de mel* broken by hand. Everyday drink: *poncha* stirred with a *caralhinho*, *nikita*, Coral beer, Brisa maracujá, Madeira wine as an aperitif. Saturday means the Mercado dos Lavradores. Use European Portuguese only, *tu* register, *estar a + infinitivo*.
