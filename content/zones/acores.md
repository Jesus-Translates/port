# Zona: Açores

## Identity

The Açores are an autonomous region (Região Autónoma dos Açores) of **nine inhabited islands** in three groups, spread across 600 km of open Atlantic. Mainland Portugal is **o continente**; flying there is *ir ao continente*, and it is a two-and-a-bit-hour flight from São Miguel, longer from the west. Roughly 240,000 people live here (2021), over half of them on **São Miguel**, with **Ponta Delgada** (about 67,229 in 2021) the largest city.

The three groups matter to how people think:

- **Grupo Oriental** — São Miguel, Santa Maria
- **Grupo Central** — Terceira, Graciosa, São Jorge, Pico, Faial
- **Grupo Ocidental** — Flores, Corvo

Islanders identify with *their* island first and the region second. A *micaelense* and a *terceirense* are different people with different accents, different festivals and a long, affectionate rivalry. Angra do Heroísmo is the historic and ecclesiastical capital; Ponta Delgada is the biggest; Horta is the third seat, and the regional government is deliberately split between all three.

Emigration runs deeper here than almost anywhere in Portugal — to Massachusetts, Rhode Island, California, Bermuda, Canada. The word **emigrante** is an everyday noun, families straddle two countries, and August is when everyone comes home.

Two other things define the place: **the volcano**, which is not a metaphor — Furnas cooks lunch with it, Pico is made of it, Capelinhos rearranged Faial with it in 1957–58 — and **the cow**. Dairy is the economy, and cattle being walked along a road at four in the afternoon is not a rural curiosity, it is traffic.

## Geography & climate

Nine volcanic islands on the mid-Atlantic ridge, where three tectonic plates meet. **Ponta do Pico**, 2,351 m, is the highest point in all of Portugal and the third-highest mountain rising from the Atlantic floor.

Highlights a resident knows by heart: the twin lakes in the **Sete Cidades** caldera and the **Lagoa do Fogo** on São Miguel; the **Vale das Furnas** with its **caldeiras** and **fumarolas**, where boiling water and mud come up through the ground; **Algar do Carvão**, a volcanic chimney you can walk down into on Terceira; the **Caldeira do Faial**, a near-circular crater about 2,000 m across; the **Vulcão dos Capelinhos**, which erupted through 1957–58 and left a moonscape and an emigration wave.

**São Jorge** is 53 km long and 8 km wide, with north-coast cliffs 300–400 m high — and at their feet, the **fajãs**, small flat shelves of land made by landslip or lava, nearly all inhabited and nearly all awkward to reach. **Pico** is black lava, walled into the vineyard **currais** whose combined stone walls would go round the equator roughly twice.

The climate is the daily subject. Mild, humid, wet, and above all **changeable** — four seasons in an afternoon is not a joke, it is a planning constraint. **Nevoeiro** (fog) is the thing that ruins arrangements: it closes airports, it hides Pico from Horta for a week, and then one morning the mountain is simply there again, enormous, and everyone comments on it. Summers are warm rather than hot; winters are wet and windy and rarely cold. **Hortênsias** — blue hydrangeas — line the roads and field boundaries from June, which is why Faial is called the **ilha Azul**. Whales and dolphins are offshore all year, which is ordinary here.

## Landmarks & buildings

**São Miguel:** the **Portas da Cidade** and **Campo de São Francisco** in Ponta Delgada; **Forte de São Brás** (1552 onward); **Portas do Mar**, the modern waterfront with the marina, cruise terminal and sea pools; the **Coliseu Micaelense** and **Teatro Micaelense**; **Parque Terra Nostra** in Furnas, one of the great 18th-century gardens of São Miguel, with a warm iron-rich bathing pool that stains your swimsuit orange and is worth it; **Poça da Dona Beija**, the hot pools open late into the evening; **Caldeira Velha** on the road to Lagoa do Fogo; the **Miradouro da Vista do Rei** over Sete Cidades; the **Ilhéu de Vila Franca do Campo**, a drowned volcanic cone with a perfect round lagoon inside, a nature reserve since 1993; the **Ponte dos Oito Arcos** (1888–1893) in Ribeira Grande; the **Fábrica de Chá Gorreana** and Porto Formoso tea factories.

**Terceira:** the whole **Zona Central da Cidade de Angra do Heroísmo**, UNESCO World Heritage since 7 December 1983; **Monte Brasil**, the volcanic peninsula closing the bay; the **Fortaleza de São João Baptista**; the **Jardim Duque da Terceira**; **Algar do Carvão**; **Base das Lajes**, the airport and air base; and the **impérios**, small brightly painted Holy Spirit chapels standing at village crossroads all over the island.

**Faial:** the **Marina da Horta**, where visiting crews paint a mural on the quay wall before they leave — a superstition as much as a tradition; **Monte da Guia**; the Capelinhos lighthouse half-buried in ash.

**Pico:** the **Paisagem da Cultura da Vinha da Ilha do Pico**, UNESCO World Heritage since 2004 — the *currais*; the whaling **vigias**, the little lookout huts on the cliffs from which spotters once called the boats out; the whaling museums at Lajes.

**São Jorge:** **Fajã dos Cubres** and **Fajã da Caldeira de Santo Cristo**, the latter with the only naturally occurring **amêijoas** in the Azores.

## Churches & religious sites

The **Diocese de Angra**, founded 1534, covers all nine islands; the **Sé de Angra do Heroísmo** is its cathedral.

The single greatest religious event in the region is the **Festas do Senhor Santo Cristo dos Milagres** in Ponta Delgada. The image — a Renaissance *Ecce Homo* carving of Christ after the flagellation, crowned with thorns — is kept at the **Convento de Nossa Senhora da Esperança**. The festivities fall around the **fifth Sunday after Easter**, ending on Ascension Thursday. On the Saturday devotees pay homage at **Campo de São Francisco**, many **de joelhos no chão**; on the Sunday the procession crosses the city over elaborate flower carpets, with the convent and the *campo* lit up. It is described as the largest and oldest devotion in the country, and thousands of emigrants time their return home to it.

The **Culto do Espírito Santo** is the other pillar, and it is everywhere, especially on **Terceira**. Fifty days after Easter is Pentecost, and the cycle runs from Easter through to the **Domingo do Bodo**, the seventh Sunday. The **coroa** — the imperial crown, with the dove — is placed on a throne dressed in white paper and flowers for a week; the priest performs the **coroação**, setting the crown on the head of the **imperador**; a **cortejo** carries the crown back to his house, where neighbours gather every evening. At Pentecost the **bodo** distributes **esmolas** — meat, bread and wine — to the brotherhood and to those in need. The ritual meal is the **sopas do Espírito Santo**: dry bread soaked in the broth the meat was boiled in, seasoned with **hortelã**, with the **massa sovada** alongside. Most masonry **impérios** date from the second half of the 19th century; before that, temporary wooden **treatros** were built each year.

The **romeiros quaresmais** of São Miguel walk the whole way round the island in about seven days during Lent, in groups, in the rain, singing as they go, stopping at church after church. Hearing a group of romeiros come round a corner singing in the dark is one of the defining São Miguel experiences.

Other sites: **Igreja Matriz de São Sebastião**, Ponta Delgada; **Igreja Matriz de Nossa Senhora da Estrela**, Ribeira Grande; the **Ermida de Nossa Senhora da Paz** above Vila Franca do Campo, at the top of a long zigzag stair.

## Food & drink

**Cozido das Furnas** — properly *cozido nas caldeiras*. A tall pot is packed with beef, pork, chicken, *chouriço*, *morcela*, cabbage, carrot, potato and **inhame** (Azorean yam), wrapped, lowered into a numbered hole in the hot volcanic ground beside the **Lagoa das Furnas**, covered over in the morning and dug out at lunchtime. Meat needs roughly **five to six hours**; fish about three to four. The ground does the cooking, and it gives the food a faint sulphurous sweetness that people either adore or diplomatically decline. Restaurants book the holes and bury their pots at dawn.

**Alcatra** — Terceira's dish: beef (or, older, *alcatra de peixe*) slow-cooked for hours in a **alguidar de barro**, an unglazed clay pan, with red wine, onion, bacon, allspice and **pimenta-da-terra**, until it falls apart. Eaten with **massa sovada** (unverified in fine detail — Wikipedia PT has no dedicated article).

**Sopas do Espírito Santo** — the festival soup described above.

From the sea: **lapas grelhadas** with garlic butter and lemon; **cracas**, Azorean barnacles, mostly Faial and Pico, which you crack and pick with a fork and which taste of concentrated sea; **chicharros**, small horse mackerel, fried or grilled — the genuinely everyday fish, cheap and eaten at home; **polvo guisado em vinho de cheiro**; **caldeirada**; and **atum** and **bonito** fresh in season.

From the land: Azorean beef, which is grass-fed year-round because the grass never stops; **morcela** and **linguiça**; **torresmos**; **inhame** with the cozido and at Easter; and **ananás dos Açores/São Miguel DOP**, pineapple grown slowly under glass in Fajã de Baixo and Ponta Delgada, PDO since 21 June 1996, managed by the **PROFRUTOS** cooperative.

Cheese is identity. **Queijo de São Jorge DOP** — protected since **1986** (Decreto Regulamentar Regional n.º 24/86/A, de 9 de julho), made only on São Jorge from **leite de vaca inteiro e cru**, cured a minimum of **three months** at 12–14 °C and 80–85% humidity, in wheels of **8–12 kg**, 25–35 cm across and 10–15 cm high, yellow paste with small irregular holes and a **bouquet forte, limpo e ligeiramente picante** that gets sharper with age. Also **Queijo do Pico**, soft and pungent, and *queijo da ilha* as a generic.

Sweet: **bolos lêvedos** from the **Vale das Furnas** — small sweet leavened discs cooked on a floured griddle, first appearing in the mid-19th century, split, buttered, and eaten with coffee at any hour; **massa sovada**; **queijadas da Vila** from Vila Franca do Campo (the **Queijadas do Morgado**); **queijadas da Graciosa**; **fofas da Povoação**; **Dona Amélia** on Terceira; **rosquilhas**.

Drink: **vinho de cheiro**, the everyday semi-wild red poured at family tables and at festas — nobody sells it in a smart bottle and everybody has an uncle who makes it (unverified as to exact grape); **verdelho** and the modern Pico wines grown in the *currais* (labels including *Frei Gigante* and *Terras de Lava*) (unverified); **Biscoitos** wine on Terceira; **Especial**, brewed by **Melo Abreu** in Ponta Delgada (unverified as to current ownership); **Kima**, the maracujá soft drink; **licor de maracujá** and **angelica**; and the tea — **Gorreana** and **Porto Formoso** on São Miguel, the only tea plantations in Portugal and the oldest in Europe, Gorreana working continuously since 1883 and producing green and black leaf.

## Restaurants & institutions

**Peter Café Sport, Horta.** Founded 1918 by Henrique Azevedo, run by the same family ever since — his son José, nicknamed **Peter**, took over in the 1960s, and José Henrique from 1978. The **gin tónico** is the house order, and it has been since Henrique made it the thing. Upstairs since 1986 is the **Museu do Peter**, a scrimshaw collection. For most of a century the café was the poste restante of the North Atlantic: crews left letters here for boats that had not arrived yet. The sailing line is *"Se velejares até à Horta e não visitares o Peter Café Sport, não viste a Horta na realidade."* The ritual: a crew comes in off an ocean crossing, still salt-stiff, orders the gin, signs something, and then goes out to paint their boat's name on the marina wall — because leaving without painting it is thought to invite a rough passage.

**The caldeiras at Furnas.** The numbered holes in the ground by the Lagoa das Furnas are the institution. The morning ritual of burying the pots draws a small crowd; the midday ritual of digging them out draws a bigger one, with steam, a hook, and a lot of unsolicited advice about whether the pot has been down long enough. Restaurants in the village are where the pot ends up — **Tony's**, on the Largo do Teatro in the middle of Furnas and trading since 1984, is the one most people name, and the restaurant at **Terra Nostra** is the other *(which house uses which hole is not something to assert)*. You book the cozido, you do not turn up hoping.

**Mercado da Graça, Ponta Delgada.** Saturday morning. Fish downstairs, pineapples piled in gold pyramids at the entrance, tea, cheese, and a very serious *chicharro* trade. The ritual is arriving early, going twice round before buying anything, and having a *bolo lêvedo* and a coffee afterwards as though it were a reward for work.

**Fábrica de Chá Gorreana**, Ribeira Grande. The old machinery still runs, the smell of drying leaf carries down the road, and there is a pot of tea on for anyone who walks in. The ritual is drinking it plain, standing up, while somebody explains that the leaf never sees a chemical because the island has no tea pests. Gorreana has been in the same family since 1883.

**Queijadas do Morgado**, Vila Franca do Campo — the *queijadas da Vila* everyone stops for on the way round the south coast.

**Coliseu Micaelense** and **Teatro Micaelense**, Ponta Delgada — where the concerts and the school galas happen.

**Mercado Municipal de Angra do Heroísmo**, and the *impérios* themselves, which double as village halls, kitchens and gossip exchanges during the festas.

Warm observations worth reusing: the standing argument over whether cozido is really cozido without the **inhame**; whether a *bolo lêvedo* should be toasted or eaten as it comes (toasting it is a modern indulgence and someone's grandmother disapproves); how sharp a **São Jorge** must be before a São Jorge person will admit it is proper — the answer is always "sharper than that"; and the way a jug of **vinho de cheiro** at a family table is poured for everyone without anyone being asked.

## Everyday life & errands

**Getting between islands is the daily fact of life.** **SATA Air Açores**, based at **Aeroporto João Paulo II** in Ponta Delgada, flies the inter-island network with **Bombardier Dash 8-Q200** (37 seats) and **Q400** (80 seats) turboprops, carrying passengers, cargo and post; the smallest strips, notably **Corvo**, need exactly this kind of aircraft. Its subsidiary **Azores Airlines** (formerly SATA Internacional) does the mainland and international routes. Fog cancels flights. People genuinely get stranded on the wrong island for two days and it is treated as weather, not catastrophe: *"ficou retido no Faial"*.

By sea, **Atlânticoline** runs the ferries, and its lines are known by **colour**, not by number — say *a linha azul*, not "line 1".

**Get this distinction right, because it is the one that strands people.** Only **one** crossing runs all year: the **linha azul**, **Horta–Madalena**, the short hop from Faial to Pico, roughly **25–30 minutes**, several sailings a day. That is the one people genuinely commute on for work, school and hospital appointments. **The full *triângulo* — the Faial–Pico–São Jorge circuit on the *linha verde* — is seasonal**, as are the lines that reach the rest of the Central group and link all nine islands: spring and summer only. So Horta to Madalena in February is a boat; **Horta or Madalena to Velas in February is not** — it is a flight, or it is nothing. Never assume São Jorge is reachable by sea out of season. Timetables are reissued each season and the winter one is far thinner: check before you count on a sailing.

Cargo comes by ship, which means shelves depend on the boat; if the sea has been bad, the supermarket says so.

**Farming** structures the week. Dairy is the backbone — cows walked along the road morning and evening, the **ordenha** twice a day, and the electric fence moved a few metres every day so the herd eats the field in strips. Milk brands processed in the region include **Lactaçores** and **Insco** (unverified as to current ownership). There is pineapple under glass, tea at Gorreana and Porto Formoso, and vines in the *currais* on Pico.

**Fishing** runs to a calendar: *chicharro* and *lapas* close in, **atum** and **bonito** in the warm months, and the auction handled through **Lotaçor** (unverified). On Pico and Faial the old whaling vocabulary survives in place names and in the **vigias** on the cliffs.

**Hot water is a utility.** Furnas has springs of different mineral waters and temperatures coming out of the ground within a few hundred metres of each other; **Poça da Dona Beija** and the **Terra Nostra** pool are places locals go on a wet Tuesday evening, not just tourists.

Institutions: the **Universidade dos Açores** (Ponta Delgada, Angra, Horta), the **Hospital do Divino Espírito Santo** in Ponta Delgada, the regional government split across the three capitals, and **Portas do Mar** for the cruise ships. Supermarkets include **SolMar** alongside the mainland chains (unverified as to current presence).

And the weather governs everything. Plans get made with *"se o tempo deixar"* attached to them as a matter of course.

## Festivals & calendar

- **January — Reis.** Groups go door to door singing the *cantar dos Reis*; on Terceira the Carnival preparations start early.
- **February/March — Carnaval, especially Terceira.** The **danças e bailinhos de Carnaval** are performed in *salões* in village after village over several nights: costumed troupes with written verses, satire and music, touring the island. It is a genuinely mass amateur-theatre tradition and nothing like a beach parade.
- **Lent — Romeiros de São Miguel.** Men walk round the island in about seven days, singing, stopping at churches.
- **March/April — Páscoa**, which starts the Espírito Santo cycle.
- **Fifth Sunday after Easter — Festas do Senhor Santo Cristo dos Milagres**, Ponta Delgada. The biggest week of the Azorean year: procession, flower carpets, illuminations, emigrants home, the fairground on the Campo de São Francisco.
- **Pentecost (50 days after Easter) and the following weeks — Festas do Espírito Santo** across the islands, especially Terceira: **coroação**, **cortejo**, **bodo**, **sopas**, running to the **Domingo do Bodo**.
- **Segunda-feira do Espírito Santo — Dia da Região Autónoma dos Açores**, the regional holiday.
- **June — Sanjoaninas**, Angra do Heroísmo, around **São João on 24 June**: parades, the *caravela Vera Cruz* on the water, bullfights, marches and a week of nights out. Terceira's **touradas à corda** run through the summer: four bulls of the *brava da ilha Terceira* breed, each held on a rope by ten **pastores**, along a street of at most 500 m, with horns covered, **foguetes** signalling release and recall, and *capinhas* working the bull with an umbrella or a *samarra*.
- **July/August — Cais de Agosto** on Pico, **Semana Cultural das Velas** on São Jorge, **Maré de Agosto** on Santa Maria, and *festas do emigrante* everywhere. August is when the diaspora comes home and the islands are at their fullest.
- **August — Semana do Mar**, Horta: sailing, music, and the marina at its most crowded.
- **September — Festas de Nossa Senhora dos Milagres** and the *vindima* on Pico.
- **December — Natal**, the *cantar das janeiras* season beginning, and the *matança do porco* still done in some villages.

## Language notes

Azorean speech is European Portuguese and everything the learner is taught — *tu*, *pequeno-almoço*, *estar a + infinitivo* — is correct here. What differs is the sound, and on São Miguel the difference is dramatic. **Micaelense is one of the most distinctive accents in the Portuguese-speaking world**, and a learner who has only heard Lisbon Portuguese will need it explained before they will believe it is the same language.

**The famous fronted vowels (São Miguel):**

- `tu` — the /u/ is realised as a **near-front rounded vowel [ʏ]**, produced with the lips rounded but the tongue forward — much closer to French *tu* or German *über* than to Lisbon's back [u]. The process is explicitly compared to the one that produced the French *u*.
- `Portugal` — the stressed *u* fronts the same way; this one word identifies a micaelense in a sentence.
- `lua` — same fronting; the vowel sits forward in the mouth.
- `outra` — the /o/ of the *ou* spelling is realised as the **front rounded [ø]**, like French *eu* in *deux*.
- `boi` — the *oi* likewise comes out as [ø] rather than a back vowel.

These two phonemes, **[ʏ]** and **[ø]**, are emblematic of Micaelense above all, and they are **phonologically unique in the Portuguese-speaking world** — no other variety has them. They appear partially in **Terceira, Graciosa, eastern Pico, Flores and Corvo**, and are **entirely absent** in **Santa Maria, Faial, São Jorge and western Pico** — which is why "the Azorean accent" is a fiction: there are nine of them, and a Faial speaker sounds far closer to the mainland than a São Miguel speaker does.

**Two features shared with Madeira**, and worth knowing because they mark a speaker as an islander rather than as a micaelense specifically:

- **/l/ palatalises after /i/** — `família` → *famílhia*, `vila` → *vilha*, `aquilo` → *aquilho*. Instant, reliable island identification and completely transparent once explained.
- **Nasal diphthongs simplify** — `-ões` leans towards *-ôns*, `-ães` towards *-âns*.

**Prosody:** Azorean speech is generally heard by mainlanders as slower and more sung, with longer vowels and a rise-and-fall that carries across a field. Micaelense in particular is often said to be hard for mainlanders on first hearing (unverified as a documented claim, though universally reported anecdotally). Azoreans in turn describe mainland speech as clipped and swallowed.

**Grammar:** the islands are reported to use the **gerúndio** (*estou fazendo*) somewhat more freely than Lisbon does, alongside the standard *estar a + infinitivo* — firmly documented for Madeira, less firmly for the Azores. This one carries a trap, because *estou fazendo* is **also** the standard Brazilian form. Where an islander says it, it is genuine southern European Portuguese and not a Brazilianism, and it must never be corrected. The learner should still produce *estar a + infinitivo*, because in a foreign mouth the gerund will be heard as Brazilian.

Everything in this section is **recognition, not production**. The target is one neutral European Portuguese; copying these features reads as mimicry, while using the local *vocabulary* below reads as belonging. The cross-zone atlas in `sotaques.md` carries the full picture.

**Lexicon — landscape and place:**

- `fajã` — a flat shelf of land at the foot of a sea cliff, made by landslip or lava; usually inhabited, always awkward to reach.
- `canada` — a narrow country lane between walls; it is a real address element on São Miguel, not a nickname.
- `grota` — a steep ravine or gully cut by water.
- `biscoito` — a field of rough, broken lava; also a place name on Terceira and its wine region.
- `mistério` — an area covered by a historical lava flow, on Pico (*Mistérios da Prainha*).
- `curral` — a small vineyard plot walled in black basalt on Pico; plural **currais**.
- `caldeira` — a volcanic crater; also, in Furnas, one of the boiling springs.
- `fumarola` — a steam vent.
- `ilhéu` — an islet off the coast.
- `vigia` — a whalers' cliff-top lookout hut.
- `rocha` — the cliff, as a general term for the coast's edge.

**Lexicon — life, food and faith:**

- `império` — the small painted Holy Spirit chapel, and the brotherhood that runs it.
- `coroação` — the crowning of the *imperador* with the Holy Spirit crown.
- `bodo` — the distribution of *esmolas* (meat, bread, wine) at Pentecost.
- `sopas do Espírito Santo` — dry bread soaked in meat broth with *hortelã*.
- `massa sovada` — the sweet enriched bread that goes with them.
- `romeiro` — a Lenten pilgrim walking round São Miguel.
- `chicharro` — small horse mackerel; the everyday fish.
- `lapas` — limpets.
- `cracas` — barnacles, mostly Faial and Pico.
- `inhame` — Azorean yam, boiled with the cozido.
- `vinho de cheiro` — the everyday homemade red poured at family tables.
- `ordenha` — the milking.
- `nevoeiro` — fog; the word that cancels flights.
- `nortada` — the persistent north wind.
- `o continente` — mainland Portugal; *ir ao continente* means flying there.
- `emigrante` — the relative in Massachusetts or Toronto; an entirely neutral, everyday word.

## Towns

### Ponta Delgada (São Miguel)

Known for: the region's largest city, about 67,229 residents (2021) across 24 *freguesias*, made a city by charter of D. João III on 2 April 1546. Landmark: the **Portas da Cidade** arches on the seafront and the **Convento de Nossa Senhora da Esperança** behind Campo de São Francisco, home of the Senhor Santo Cristo. Food detail: the **Mercado da Graça** on a Saturday morning — *chicharro*, pineapple, São Jorge cheese — and a *bolo lêvedo* afterwards. Everyday life: the **João Paulo II** airport, **Portas do Mar** for the ferries and cruise ships, the **Hospital do Divino Espírito Santo**, the **Universidade dos Açores**, and the **Coliseu Micaelense** for everything from concerts to school prize-givings.

### Furnas (Povoação, São Miguel)

Known for: living on top of an active volcano and cooking with it. Landmark: the **caldeiras** by the **Lagoa das Furnas** — geysers of boiling water and medicinal mud rising from the ground — and the **Parque Terra Nostra**, one of the finest 18th-century gardens on the island. Food detail: **cozido das Furnas** buried in a numbered hole at dawn and dug out at lunch (5–6 hours for meat, 3–4 for fish), and **bolos lêvedos**, which were born here in the mid-19th century. Everyday life: a village with dozens of mineral springs of different tastes and temperatures within walking distance, where people bathe in the **Poça da Dona Beija** or the orange Terra Nostra pool on ordinary weeknights.

### Ribeira Grande (São Miguel)

Known for: the north coast, and surf. Landmark: the **Ponte dos Oito Arcos**, built 1888–1893, in the historic centre, and the **Igreja Matriz de Nossa Senhora da Estrela**. Food detail: the **Fábrica de Chá Gorreana** just along the coast, where the old machinery still runs; local *queijadas* and *licores*. Everyday life: Santa Bárbara beach for the surfers, the *Casa do Arcano* and the emigration museum, and the drive up to **Caldeira Velha** and **Lagoa do Fogo** whenever the cloud lifts.

### Vila Franca do Campo (São Miguel)

Known for: having been the island's most important settlement until the **subversão de Vila Franca** on the night of **21–22 October 1522**, the earthquake and mudslide that buried most of the town and killed thousands here and in settlements across the island. Landmark: the **Ilhéu de Vila Franca do Campo**, a drowned cone with a circular lagoon inside, a nature reserve since 1993, and the **Ermida de Nossa Senhora da Paz** at the top of its zigzag staircase. Food detail: **queijadas da Vila**, specifically the **Queijadas do Morgado**. Everyday life: the boat out to the ilhéu in summer, and the long view back over the town from the ermida that everyone climbs at least once a year.

### Angra do Heroísmo (Terceira)

Known for: the **Zona Central**, UNESCO World Heritage since 7 December 1983, and being the ecclesiastical capital — the **Diocese de Angra** dates from 1534. Population about 33,771 (2021). Landmark: **Monte Brasil** closing the bay, with the **Fortaleza de São João Baptista** on its neck, and the **Jardim Duque da Terceira** in the middle of town. Food detail: **alcatra** in a clay *alguidar*, and **Dona Amélia** cakes. Everyday life: the **Sanjoaninas** in June around São João on the 24th; **touradas à corda** through the summer; **danças de Carnaval** through February; and the *impérios* standing painted at village crossroads all year round.

### Horta (Faial)

Known for: being the Atlantic's crossroads. Landmark: the **Marina da Horta** and its painted quay wall, and **Peter Café Sport** (1918) with the **Museu do Peter** upstairs. Food detail: **cracas** picked out of the shell with a fork, and the house **gin tónico**. Everyday life: the **Horta–Madalena** ferry across to Pico in about half an hour — the one Azorean crossing that runs all year — so people cross for work, school and appointments, while the boat on to **São Jorge** is a summer thing only; **Monte da Guia** for the walk; the **Caldeira do Faial**, about 2,000 m across, up in the cloud; and the **Semana do Mar** in August. Faial is the **ilha Azul** for its hydrangeas.

### Madalena (Pico)

Known for: the ferry port facing Horta across the channel, and the wine. Landmark: the **currais** — the UNESCO **Paisagem da Cultura da Vinha da Ilha do Pico**, inscribed in 2004, whose black basalt walls would circle the equator about twice — and **Ponta do Pico**, 2,351 m, the highest point in Portugal, standing over the town. Food detail: **verdelho** from the *currais*, and *vinho de cheiro* at home. Everyday life: crossing to Horta for anything the island does not have; the mountain being visible or not visible defining the mood of the day.

### Velas (São Jorge)

Known for: cheese. **Queijo de São Jorge DOP** is the island's principal product — raw whole cow's milk, cured at least 90 days, wheels of 8–12 kg, *forte, limpo e ligeiramente picante*. Landmark: the **fajãs** along the north coast — **Fajã dos Cubres**, **Fajã da Caldeira de Santo Cristo** with the only wild **amêijoas** in the Azores. Food detail: the cheese, eaten in thick slabs with bread and *vinho de cheiro*, and the clams from the Fajã da Caldeira. Everyday life: an island 53 km long and 8 km wide with 300–400 m cliffs on the north side, so getting down to a *fajã* is a decision, not a detour; the ferry across to Pico and Faial, which is a **summer service only** — out of season São Jorge is reached by air, and planning around that is ordinary life here; and the **Semana Cultural das Velas** in July.

## Prompt context

The learner lives in the Açores — nine islands, three groups, where mainland Portugal is *o continente* and reaching it means a flight. Most people are on São Miguel; Ponta Delgada is the biggest city, Angra do Heroísmo the historic capital, Horta the Atlantic crossroads. Volcanic ground is a utility: Furnas cooks *cozido nas caldeiras* in numbered holes for five or six hours, and people bathe at the Poça da Dona Beija on a wet evening. Weather rules everything — *nevoeiro* cancels SATA flights, plans carry *se o tempo deixar*, hydrangeas line the roads in June. The Horta–Madalena ferry is a half-hour commute and the only year-round crossing — other inter-island boats, São Jorge included, are summer only; cargo comes by ship. Dairy is the economy: cows on the road, the *ordenha* twice a day, *queijo de São Jorge* aged until it bites. Everyday food: *chicharros*, *lapas*, *cracas*, *inhame*, *bolo lêvedo* split and buttered, *alcatra* on Terceira, *sopas do Espírito Santo* at the *império*, *vinho de cheiro* on the table. The year turns on the Senhor Santo Cristo in Ponta Delgada and the Espírito Santo everywhere. Use European Portuguese only, *tu* register, *estar a + infinitivo*.
