# Sotaques e dialetos de Portugal

A cross-zone dossier. The individual zone files carry short **Language notes** for the place a
learner lives; this file is the atlas behind them — what the country actually sounds like, why,
and which of it a learner should copy rather than merely understand.

One principle governs the whole document. **Recognition and production are different skills.**
A learner needs to *understand* a Minho farmer, a micaelense fisherman and an Alentejano
neighbour. A learner needs to *produce* one consistent, neutral European Portuguese. Confusing
the two produces someone who sounds like a bad impression of wherever they last had coffee.

---

## The standard, and who actually speaks it

Standard European Portuguese is conventionally defined as *"o conjunto dos usos linguísticos das
classes cultas da região Lisboa–Coimbra"* — the usage of the educated classes of the
Lisbon–Coimbra region. It is worth knowing that this definition is **circular and slightly
fictional**, and that Portuguese linguists say so openly: that "region" has never existed as a
geographical or sociolinguistic unit. Coimbra's place in the formula comes largely from the
prestige of its university rather than from any dialectological fact.

In practice the reference is **educated Lisbon speech**. Aniceto dos Reis Gonçalves Viana, who
wrote the foundational description of Portuguese pronunciation, acknowledged a *padrão médio*
somewhere between Coimbra and Lisbon but described the standard on the basis of how educated
Lisboners actually spoke. In Cintra's dialect scheme the relevant variety is **estremenho**, and
its **lisboeta** sub-variety is what the norm was built from. The standard is therefore a
*centro-meridional* variety — a southern one. This matters more than it sounds: the prestige
norm of Portuguese is, historically, a **southern innovation that won**.

**Sources disagree about where the norm stops and Lisbon starts.** Ciberdúvidas has answered the
question both ways. One answer states that the norm *"absorveu muitos traços dialectais da
capital"* — absorbed many of the capital's dialect traits, including the [ɐ] vowel in *coelho*.
Another answer, on a different day, calls *coâlho* and *riu* *"particularidade da fala lisboeta,
e não a norma"* — a particularity of Lisbon speech, not the norm — and points to Coimbra as the
historic seat of correctness. The Observatório da Língua Portuguesa, scoring accents on
consonants, vowels, diphthongs and sonority, lands on **Coimbra as the most neutral**. There is
no settled answer. This dossier treats the safe core as: the features every educated speaker
shares, minus the ones that are audibly *lisboeta*.

**How many people speak it unmarked?** Very few, and the honest answer is that nobody has a
number. Standard EP is a *register*, not a birthplace — it is what a newsreader, a teacher or a
lawyer switches into. Almost every Portuguese person carries something regional: an /s/ that is
a shade too apical, an *ou* that refuses to flatten, a nasal that runs long. Ciberdúvidas notes
that tolerance for regional pronunciation has grown, *"mesmo no registo formal"* — even in
formal register. The learner's target is therefore not "how Portuguese people speak". It is
**the unmarked register they can all switch into**, which is a narrower and more attainable
thing.

## The big divide

The standard reference is **Luís Filipe Lindley Cintra, *Nova proposta de classificação dos
dialectos galego-portugueses*** (Boletim de Filologia XXII, 1964/1971, pp. 81–116; reissued in
*Estudos de Dialectologia Portuguesa*, Sá da Costa, 1983). Cintra built it from his own fieldwork
for the **Atlas Lingüístico de la Península Ibérica (ALPI)** in 1953–54, where he and the
Galician researcher Aníbal Otero surveyed 72 Portuguese points between them. Cintra described
his own method as *"uma consciente e voluntária tentativa de simplificação"* — a deliberate
attempt at simplification, resting on a small, ranked set of phonetic traits.

He used **five isoglosses** to divide the Portuguese dialects internally (two further ones
separated Portuguese from Galician):

1. **Ausência de distinção fonológica entre /b/ e /v/**, in favour of /b/ — realised as [b] or
   [β] depending on context. (Betacismo.)
2. **Realização apical dos fonemas fricativos alveolares**, voiced and voiceless.
3. **Conservação do fonema africado pós-alveolar surdo [tʃ]** — the old *tch* of `ch`.
4. **Conservação do ditongo `ou`.**
5. **Conservação do ditongo `ei`.**

### Where the line runs

The top-level split — **dialectos setentrionais** versus **dialectos centro-meridionais** — is
drawn on **criterion 2, the apical sibilants**. Cintra's isogloss runs, roughly, from **between
the districts of Porto and Aveiro across to the Spanish border at Idanha-a-Nova**, with a small
island of absence in the coastal strip of the Braga district.

Two things follow that surprise people. First, the line is **not** the Douro and **not** the
"Norte/Sul" of everyday talk — it sits well south of Porto on the coast and rises inland, so
that **Beira Alta (Guarda, the high interior) is dialectally northern** while Beira Litoral is
not. Drive the A25 from Aveiro to Guarda and you cross it. Second, the divide is defined by a
feature — apical *s* — that most learners never consciously notice.

### The two sides

**Dialectos setentrionais**, subdivided by their sibilant inventory:

- **Transmontanos and alto-minhotos** keep the **archaic four-sibilant system** — a voiceless
  apico-alveolar [s̺], its voiced partner [z̺], and a voiceless and voiced predorsodental [s]
  and [z]. Spelling still tracks it: `passo` and `paço` are not homophones.
- **Baixo-minhotos, durienses and beirões** reduced that to **two**, keeping only the
  apico-alveolar series.

Beyond sibilants, the northern group is the one that **merges /b/ and /v/**, **keeps `ou` and
`ei` as real diphthongs**, and — in the Alto Minho and Trás-os-Montes — **keeps the [tʃ]
affricate** distinct from [ʃ].

**Dialectos centro-meridionais** neutralised the sibilant opposition **in favour of the
predorsodentals** — and, in Cintra's words, that is *"o modelo que foi adoptado como referência
para a ortofonia do português padrão."* The standard's /s/ is a southern /s/. This group keeps
**/b/ and /v/ distinct**, **monophthongises `ou`**, and is itself split in two on criterion 5:

- **Centro-litoral** — keeps the `ei` diphthong.
- **Centro-interior e sul** — monophthongises it.

The named dialects usually listed are, in the north, **alto-minhoto**, **baixo-minhoto-duriense**
and **transmontano** (with **beirão** straddling); in the centre-south, **estremenho** (with
**lisboeta** and **coimbrão** sub-varieties), **baixo-beirão / alto-alentejano**, **alentejano**
and **algarvio**; and the insular **açoriano** and **madeirense**, grouped with the
centro-meridionais.

### Where the classification is contested

Cintra's scheme is the field's shared vocabulary, and this dossier uses it. It is also, fifty
years on, **demonstrably rough at the edges**. Xosé Afonso Álvarez Pérez (Centro de Linguística
da Universidade de Lisboa) re-analysed the unpublished ALPI materials quantitatively — 299
questions, **49,673 responses**, 90 network points — and published the results as *"Apontamentos
sobre a consistência da Nova proposta de classificação dos dialectos galego-portugueses de
Lindley Cintra"* (APL, Textos Selecionados do XXIX Encontro Nacional, Porto, 2014, pp. 43–56).
What he found:

- **The apical sibilants — the "coluna dorsal" of the whole classification — are missing across
  a large stretch of the north-western littoral**, far larger than Cintra marked. Álvarez Pérez
  concludes that there is no justification for putting the west of the Viana do Castelo and Braga
  districts, or the north of Porto, in the setentrional group at all, since the defining trait is
  *"praticamente inexistente naquela zona"* — practically non-existent there. His verdict on the
  criterion: *"a escolha deste fenómeno linguístico como facto diferenciador de dialectos não é
  totalmente bem-sucedida."*
- **The `ei` and `ou` diphthongs survive well south of Cintra's lines.** `ei` was diphthongal in
  **65.79%** of 3,932 relevant responses; only five points in the whole network monophthongised
  it completely. `ou` held at **46.01%**. Points at Famalicão (Aveiro) and Chainça (Coimbra) —
  south of the line — ran at 46% and 70%.
- **There is a pocket of `ei` conservation around Lisbon itself**, surveyed in 1936, which Cintra
  had documented in 1970 but left off the *Nova proposta* map.
- **There is an island in the district of Lisbon where /b/ and /v/ are not distinguished** —
  betacismo, in the capital's own district. Cintra reported it in a 1961 paper; it is absent from
  the *Nova proposta* map and text.
- **The [tʃ] affricate is patchier than mapped.** Present at 50% of points, but averaging 69.47%
  vitality and fully consistent in only ten localities. Cintra excluded the coastal Braga and
  Porto districts; the ALPI data show four of six excluded points had affricates.

The structural criticism is the important one: **Cintra's isoglosses are binary.** They draw a
line with the feature inside and absent outside, and cannot represent the fact that real dialect
boundaries are gradients, varying by speaker age, sex and social layer within a single village.
Read the map as a sketch of tendencies, never as a border.

One more caveat worth carrying: the [tʃ] affricate is described as *"a característica dialectal
mais estigmatizada socialmente no português"* — speakers who have it suppress it in formal
contexts, which means fieldwork systematically under-records it. Cintra's own note from Tamente,
Ponte da Barca: the informant answered without it, but it came back in free conversation.

## Features, one by one

Each entry gives what it is, where it occurs, a plain example, and the verdict — **RECOGNISE**
(understand it, never produce it) or **ADOPT** (make it part of your own speech).

### Betacismo — the /b/–/v/ merger

**What.** /v/ is not a separate phoneme; both spellings come out bilabial, as a stop [b] between
pauses and a softer fricative [β] between vowels.

**Where.** Cintra's criterion 1, and the most robust of the five: in the ALPI data, **44.97%** of
relevant forms were bilabial, with some record at **85.55%** of network points; restricting to
points with real vitality still gives **54.44%** of the territory at **79.25%** average. Strongest
in the **Minho** and rural **Braga**, general across the northern interior and Trás-os-Montes,
weaker in urban Porto and among younger and schooled speakers. Also, separately, in
**Barranquenho** — and in that documented island in the **district of Lisbon**.

**Example.** `vinho` → *binho*. `vaca` → *baca*. `vem` → *bem*.

**Verdict. RECOGNISE.** Never adopt. It is affectionately mocked nationally and a learner doing
it sounds like a caricature. But do not hear it as an error either — it is the oldest and
best-attested dialect boundary in the country.

### The `ou` diphthong

**What.** Whether `ou` is a real falling diphthong [ow] (sometimes closer to *âu*) or a single
vowel [o].

**Where.** Kept as a diphthong in *"parte do Centro-Norte e praticamente todo o Norte"*. The
monophthong [o] **originated in southern popular Portuguese**, spread into the prestige speech
of Lisbon and Coimbra, and is now the standard. Ciberdúvidas is explicit that conserving the
diphthong *"é perfeitamente correcto"* — this is a case where the non-standard form is not
substandard.

**Example.** `ouro` → *ôro* (standard) vs *ouro* with the glide intact (north). Also `pouco`,
`outro`, `roupa`, `sou`.

**Verdict. ADOPT the monophthong** [o] as the neutral default. **RECOGNISE** the diphthong, and
know that a northern speaker using it is not making a mistake.

### The `ei` diphthong — three-way, not two-way

**What.** The single most useful accent diagnostic in Portugal, because it has **three** regional
outcomes rather than two.

**Where and how.**

- **North:** kept as [ej]. `dinheiro`, `primeiro`, `deixei` with the glide audible.
- **Centre-interior and south** (Alentejo, Algarve, and the *saloio* belt around Lisbon):
  monophthongised to **[e]**. `leite` → *lête*; `isqueiro` → *isquêro*; `galinheiro` →
  *galinhêro*; `maneira` → *manêra*.
- **Lisbon:** neither — it becomes **[ɐj]**, the *â* of *tâmara* plus a glide. `feira`, `maneira`
  and `deixei` come out as *fâirâ*, *mânâirâ*, *dâixâi* — [fˈɐjɾɐ], [mɐnˈɐjɾɐ], [dɐjʃˈɐj].

That Lisbon [ɐj] is the sound most foreign learners are actually taught, usually without being
told it is a Lisbon feature. Two villages outside Lisbon and it becomes *manêra*.

**Verdict. ADOPT [ɐj]** — it is what learning materials, dictionaries and Portuguese teachers
model, and it will not mark anyone as a poser. **RECOGNISE** [ej] in the north and [e] in the
south. Sandra should never "correct" *lête*.

### Unstressed vowel reduction

**What.** The defining rhythm of European Portuguese. Unstressed /a e o/ collapse to **[ɐ], [ɨ],
[u]** — and in fast speech the [ɨ] is frequently **deleted outright**, stacking consonants
together. The Portuguese phonology literature puts it plainly: *"as vogais átonas sofrem redução
frequente ou até mesmo cancelamento."*

**Where.** All of European Portuguese, strongest in Lisbon and the south, notably weaker in the
north — northern speakers give unstressed vowels more body, which is why northern speech sounds
slower and rounder and Lisbon sounds clipped and consonantal.

**Example.** `felizes` → [fɨˈlizɨʃ]. `Filipa` squeezed towards *Flipa*. `pequeno-almoço` with the
first *e* barely there.

**Verdict. ADOPT — non-negotiable.** This is the feature that separates European from Brazilian
Portuguese more than any other, and the one that most reliably makes a learner intelligible to
Portuguese ears. A learner who pronounces every vowel fully sounds Brazilian, regardless of
vocabulary.

### The [tʃ] affricate

**What.** Medieval Portuguese contrasted an affricate `ch` [tʃ] with a fricative `x` [ʃ], from
different etymological sources. Most of the country merged them from the seventeenth century on;
the spelling still records the old distinction.

**Where.** Cintra's criterion 3: **Alto Minho and Trás-os-Montes**, as in Galicia — plus, per the
ALPI re-analysis, parts of coastal Braga and Porto that Cintra excluded. Present at half the
network points but consistent at only ten. Heavily stigmatised and actively suppressed in formal
speech, so it is more alive than any survey shows.

**Example.** `chave` [tʃave] does not rhyme with `xaile` [ʃajlɨ]. `chorar`, `chamar`, `macho`,
`sacho`.

**Verdict. RECOGNISE only.** Never adopt, and never comment on it — it carries real social sting.

### Final `-e` retention and paragoge

**What.** Standard EP reduces final unstressed `-e` to [ɨ] and usually swallows it entirely.
Several varieties instead **keep it audible**, or **add a vowel after a final consonant**
(paragoge).

**Where.** **Madeira** most famously; also documented in the **Alentejo**. In **Barranquenho** the
final `-e` goes further and becomes `-i`.

**Example.** `fazer` → *fazêre* / *fazêri*. `comer` → *comêre*. `falar` → *falare*. Barranquenho
`pobre` → *pobri*.

**Verdict. RECOGNISE.** The Madeiran version is the single most caricatured island trait on the
mainland; a learner producing it in Lisbon would be heard as doing an impression.

### Palatalisation of /l/ after /i/

**What.** An /l/ following an /i/ and preceding a vowel turns into the palatal [ʎ] — the sound
written `lh`.

**Where.** **Madeira** and the **Açores**, described together in the Observatório's survey of
Portuguese accents.

**Example.** `família` → *famílhia*. `quilo` → *quilho*. `elástica` → *elhástica*. `vila` →
*vilha*. `aquilo` → *aquilho*. `Filipe` → *Filhipe*.

**Verdict. RECOGNISE.** It is instant, reliable island identification and completely transparent
once explained.

### The Azorean fronted /u/ and /o/

**What.** São Miguel fronts the back rounded vowels. /u/ becomes a **near-front rounded [ʏ]** —
lips rounded, tongue forward, much closer to French *tu* or German *über* than to Lisbon's back
[u]. The /o/ written `ou` or `oi` becomes a **front rounded [ø]**, like French *eu* in *deux*.
These two phonemes are phonologically unique in the Portuguese-speaking world; no other variety
has them.

**Where.** **São Miguel** above all. Partially present in **Terceira, Graciosa, eastern Pico,
Flores and Corvo**; **entirely absent** in **Santa Maria, Faial, São Jorge and western Pico** —
which is why "the Azorean accent" is a fiction. A Faial speaker sounds far closer to the mainland
than a micaelense does.

**Example.** `tu`, `Portugal`, `lua` with [ʏ]; `outra`, `boi` with [ø]. The word `Portugal` alone
identifies a micaelense in a sentence.

**Verdict. RECOGNISE.** This is the feature most likely to make a learner think they are hearing
a different language. Explain it before they hear it, not after.

### The Alentejo (and southern) gerúndio

**What.** Standard EP forms the progressive with **estar a + infinitivo**: *estou a fazer*, *ela
está a comer*. Much of the south and the islands uses the **gerúndio** instead: *estou fazendo*,
*ela está comendo*.

**Where.** The **Alentejo**, the **Algarve**, **Madeira** (marked preference), and the **Açores**
(reported, less firmly documented). Grammatically this is the centro-meridional group's
construction, described in the dialect literature as the way southern progressives are formed.

**Example.** `estou a fazer` (standard) / `estou fazendo` (Alentejo, Madeira).

**Verdict. RECOGNISE — and this is the one that carries a trap.** *Estou fazendo* is also the
standard Brazilian form. It is **not a Brazilianism when an Alentejano says it**; it is an
independent, genuine southern European Portuguese feature with its own history. A learner must
**produce *estar a + infinitivo*** — because in a learner's mouth the gerund will be heard as
Brazilian — while never correcting a neighbour who uses it.

### Southern and island vowel collapse (bonus)

**What.** Beyond `ei`, southern and island speech simplifies nasal diphthongs and drops final
vowels.

**Where and example.** Alentejo: `bem` → *bã*; a nasalised, lengthened emphasis at word ends.
Islands: `-ões` → *-ôns*, `-ães` → *-âns*. Algarve: final vowels drop wholesale, so *o meu irmão
comprou um carro novo* runs out as [u ˈme iɾˈmɐ̃ kõˈpɾo ũ ˈkaʁ ˈnov].

**Verdict. RECOGNISE.**

### The Algarve /z/ → /ʒ/ liaison

**What.** Where a word ending in `s` meets a following vowel, standard EP voices the sibilant to
[z]. The Algarve makes it **[ʒ]** instead.

**Where.** The **Algarve**, and occasionally **Coimbra**. Specifically **not** the Alentejo —
this is the cleanest way to tell the two southern accents apart.

**Example.** *Tás a ver?* → standard *tázaver*, Algarve **tájaver**.

**Verdict. RECOGNISE.**

### The Lisbon [ɐ] before palatals

**What.** An /e/ before a palatal or pre-palatal consonant lowers and centralises to **[ɐ]**.

**Where.** **Lisbon**, and from there into much of what is taught as the norm — this is exactly
the boundary sources disagree about (see *The standard*, above).

**Example.** `coelho` → *coâlho*. `espelho` → *espâlho*. `tenho` → *tânho*. Also `venho`, `veja`,
`fecha`. Coimbra says *coêlho*.

**Verdict. Learner's choice, leaning ADOPT** — it is what most teaching materials model, and it
is normal in the Oeste and Lisbon area where this app's family lives. Worth knowing it is a
capital feature, not a law.

### The standard consonant furniture (ADOPT, all of it)

Not dialect features — the shared spine of European Portuguese, and where learners most often
drift towards Brazilian habits:

- **Coda sibilants palatalise**: [ʃ] before a voiceless consonant or word-finally, [ʒ] before a
  voiced one. *Os livros* → *ujlivrus*-ish; *lisboetas* ends in a *sh*.
- **Dark /l/**: velarised [ɫ] in European dialects. *Portugal*, *mal*, *Brasil* — never the
  Brazilian [w].
- **Uvular /ʁ/**: the standard `rr` and initial `r` are uvular, fricative [ʁ] or trilled [ʀ].
  (An apical alveolar trill survives in conservative interior speech — Guarda, Viseu, the Serra —
  but is receding; *unverified* as to how current it still is.)
- **Northern apical /s̺/**: the whistly, faintly *sh*-tinted sibilant of the northern coast and
  Galicia. **RECOGNISE**, do not adopt.

## Minority and protected varieties

### Mirandês — a second official language, not a dialect

**Status.** Recognised by **Lei n.º 7/99, de 29 de janeiro** — *"Reconhecimento oficial de
direitos linguísticos da comunidade mirandesa."* Teaching is regulated by **Despacho Normativo
n.º 35/99, de 20 de julho**, which allows Mirandese in basic and secondary education in Miranda
do Douro as curricular enrichment — optional, one hour a week.

**What it is.** **Asturo-Leonese**, a Western Ibero-Romance language. It is **not** a dialect of
Portuguese and is not descended from it; it is Portuguese's cousin, not its child. Saying
otherwise is the single most reliable way to annoy a mirandês.

**Where.** The **Terra de Miranda**, roughly **500–550 km²** (sources vary) in the far
north-east: nearly all the villages of the **concelho de Miranda do Douro**, plus villages in
**Vimioso** — the lists differ between sources, with **Angueira** and **Vilar Seco** in all of
them and **Caçarelhos** in some. Bilingual municipal signage is normal throughout.

**Speakers.** A 2020 University of Vigo study estimated about **3,500 people with knowledge** of
the language and about **1,500 regular users**.

**Variants.** Three: **central** (or *normal*, the reference standard), **setentrional** (or
*raiano*, the border variety), and **meridional** (or *sendinês*).

**What a learner should know.** That it exists, that it is co-official, and that it is a
language. Nothing more is required. The **Pauliteiros de Miranda** stick dance and the
*gaita-de-foles* travel with it culturally.

### Barranquenho — the border mix

**Status.** Recognised by the **Assembleia da República unanimously on 26 November 2021** and
promulgated by the President on **19 December 2021**, granting the right to cultivate and promote
it, the right to learn it in local schools, and the option for public bodies in the concelho to
issue documents accompanied by a Barranquenho version. It had been municipal cultural heritage
since 2008. (Reported as **Lei n.º 97/2021, de 30 de dezembro** — *law number unverified against
the primary text in this pass*.)

**What it is.** A **mixed border variety** — Portuguese base, heavily restructured by southern
Spanish. The classification is genuinely unsettled: Wikipedia's own section heading calls it
*"dialecto misto"* while the lead calls it a dialect of Portuguese, and the ALPI re-analysis
**excluded Barrancos from its dialect counts entirely** on the grounds that it is a mixed variety
originating from Portuguese–Spanish contact.

**Where.** The **concelho de Barrancos**, district of Beja, in the far east of the Alentejo on
the Spanish border, wedged between Extremadura and Andalusia. A community of roughly 1,500
people (speaker count approximate).

**Features.** All of these are documented, and the Andalusian fingerprints are obvious:

- **Aspiration of final `s`/`z`**: `cruz` → *cruh*; `buscar` → *buhcá*.
- **Loss through aspiration**: `uma vez` → *uma bê*.
- **`j`, `ge`, `gi` as Spanish [x]**, not Portuguese [ʒ].
- **Final `-l` and `-r` dropped**: `Manuel` → *Manué*; `olivar` → *olivá*.
- **Syllable-final `l` → `r`**: `algo` → *argo*.
- **b/v merged**: `vaca` → ['baka].
- **Final `-e` → `-i`**: `pobre` → *pobri*.
- **Castilian pronouns and verbs**: *nusotrus* for `nós`; *andubi* (Sp. *anduve*); *supimus*
  (Sp. *supimos*); Castilian-pattern clitic placement rather than Portuguese.

**What a learner should know.** That a Portuguese village can sound half-Spanish and still be
speaking a protected national variety. Nothing here is transferable — do not adopt a single
feature.

### Minderico — the tailors' code that became a town's speech

**What it is.** A variety spoken in **Minde** (concelho de Alcanena) since the eighteenth
century, known locally as *"piação dos charales do Ninhou"*. It began as a **professional secret
code** among the town's blanket makers and traders and expanded outward until it served all
social contexts. ISO 639-3 code **drc**. A second, rarer variant exists at **Mira de Aire**.

**Status.** **No official recognition.** Endangered — most adults still know it, young people
increasingly do not. Documented and supported by **CIDLeS** (Centro Interdisciplinar de
Documentação Linguística e Social); five editions of a glossary exist, the fullest from 2004.

**What a learner should know.** Little, unless they live in Minde — but it is a good corrective
to the idea that Portugal's linguistic map has only two interesting corners.

### Also worth a line

**Língua Gestual Portuguesa** is Portugal's sign language and holds constitutional protection
*(unverified in this research pass)*. It is a full language, unrelated in structure to spoken
Portuguese.

## What a learner should do about it

**1. Produce one neutral standard, consistently.** Educated Lisbon-area European Portuguese, as
taught. Full unstressed vowel reduction, [ʃ]/[ʒ] coda sibilants, dark /l/, uvular /ʁ/,
monophthongised `ou`, `ei` as [ɐj], `estar a + infinitivo`. This is a *register*, and it is what
a Portuguese person switches into when they want to be understood by anyone. It is unmarked
everywhere in the country.

**2. Never adopt a regional feature you did not grow up with.** Betacismo, the [tʃ] affricate,
Madeiran *fazêre*, micaelense [ʏ] — every one of these, in a foreign learner's mouth, reads as
mimicry rather than integration. The one exception is **vocabulary**: local words for local
things (`semilha`, `monte`, `fajã`, `cimbalino`, `nortada`) are not accent, they are competence,
and using them correctly is a compliment to the place.

**3. Recognise the local variety before you need to.** Learners panic when a neighbour sounds
"wrong". Knowing in advance that `leite` will be *lête* in the Alentejo, that `vinho` may be
*binho* in the Minho, and that `Portugal` in São Miguel has a French-sounding *u*, removes the
panic entirely.

**4. Never correct a regional form.** *Lête*, *binho*, *estou fazendo*, *fazêre* are not errors.
Tolerance for regional pronunciation in Portugal has increased, including in formal register.
Correcting an Alentejano's gerund is both wrong and rude.

**5. Guard the Brazilian border deliberately — this is the real risk.** The internet is
overwhelmingly Brazilian Portuguese, and a learner absorbs it passively. The forms most likely to
leak in:

| Brazilian form | European target |
| --- | --- |
| *estou fazendo* (as a learner's default) | **estou a fazer** |
| *você* as neutral second person | **tu** with family and friends; *o senhor / a senhora* for respect |
| *café da manhã* | **pequeno-almoço** |
| *ônibus*, *trem*, *celular*, *banheiro* | **autocarro, comboio, telemóvel, casa de banho** |
| *time*, *bala*, *geladeira* | **equipa, rebuçado, frigorífico** |
| Full unstressed vowels | **Reduced [ɐ ɨ u], deleted in fast speech** |
| Final /l/ as [w] (*Brasiu*) | **Dark [ɫ]** (*Brasil*) |
| *ti*/*di* as [tʃi]/[dʒi] | **[ti]/[di]** |

The gerund is the sharpest edge here, because it is **simultaneously** a legitimate southern
European Portuguese form and the standard Brazilian one. The rule that resolves it: *produce*
`estar a + infinitivo`, *accept* the gerund from a local, and never present the gerund to a
learner as the form to aim at.

## Region-by-region quick reference

**Norte** — Dialectally setentrional and the most recognisable accent in the country, worn with
pride. Betacismo (*binho*, *baca*) in the Minho and rural Braga; `ou` and `ei` kept as full
diphthongs (*pouco*, *dinheiro*); apical, whistly /s/ along the coast and into Galicia;
unstressed vowels given their full body, so speech sounds slower and rounder than Lisbon's.
Braga's cadence is more sung than Porto's, with a rising melody. The [tʃ] affricate and surviving
*vós* forms persist inland in the Alto Minho and Trás-os-Montes. **Mirandês** is the co-official
Asturo-Leonese language of the Miranda do Douro corner.

**Centro** — The zone the dialect line cuts through. Coimbra sits inside the reference variety
and is often judged the most neutral accent in Portugal; Beira Litoral is centro-meridional. But
**Beira Alta — Guarda, Viseu, the high interior — is dialectally northern**, with apical
sibilants, betacismo, the *tch* affricate, preserved diphthongs, and an apical trilled `r` rather
than the coastal uvular. These are tendencies eroding under schooling and television.

**Oeste** — Estremenho, the same group as Lisbon, and effectively the prestige norm: /b/ and /v/
distinct, `ei` and `ou` monophthongising (*pêxe*, *ôtro*), hard unstressed reduction. There is no
strong local accent to imitate here — a learner in Torres Vedras or Caldas is already living
inside the standard. The inland belt is *região saloia*, where `ei` goes to plain **[e]**
(*manêra*) rather than Lisbon's [ɐj] (*manâira*) — the one audible difference between the town
and the villages behind it. What distinguishes the zone is vocabulary, not sound.

**Ribatejo** — Centro-meridional, transitional between the Estremenho core and the Alentejo, with
southern vowel collapse strengthening as you go south and east from the Tejo *(the fine detail of
Ribatejano is thinly documented in the sources consulted — unverified beyond the general
centro-meridional grouping)*. Culturally the *campino* and the bull, which carries its own
vocabulary rather than its own phonology.

**Lisboa** — The base of the norm, and simultaneously a marked local accent, which is the
paradox at the centre of this file. `ei` → **[ɐj]** (*fâirâ*, *mânâirâ*); /e/ → **[ɐ]** before
palatals (*coâlho*, *espâlho*, *tânho*); the hardest unstressed-vowel reduction and outright
deletion in the country, giving the clipped, consonantal texture foreigners hear as "swallowed".
Predorsodental sibilants. And a documented island of **betacismo** somewhere in the district,
reported by Cintra in 1961 and quietly left off his own map.

**Setúbal** — Centro-meridional and functionally continuous with Lisbon across the river, with
southern features strengthening south and east towards the Alentejo boundary *(unverified as a
separately described dialect area; sources treat it inside the Estremenho/Alentejano
transition)*.

**Alentejo** — One of the three instantly identifiable accents. The defining feature is
**prosody**: slow, *arrastado*, with lengthened open vowels and a nasalised emphasis at word ends
that the whole country imitates. `ei` → **ê** (*lête*, *galinhêro*, *isquêro*); `bem` → *bã*;
documented paragoge (*fazêri*). The **gerúndio** (*estou fazendo*) is ordinary here, and is not a
Brazilianism. Strong preference for *a gente* over *nós*. Crucially, **the Alentejo does not do
the Algarve's /z/ → /ʒ/**. The dialect area extends across the border into **Olivença** and
**Táliga**. **Barranquenho** sits in its far eastern corner.

**Algarve** — Centro-meridional, sharing most of the Alentejo's vowel collapse but sounding
completely different because it is **fast, flat and swallowed** rather than dragged. Final vowels
drop wholesale (*o meu irmão comprou um carro novo* → [u ˈme iɾˈmɐ̃ kõˈpɾo ũ ˈkaʁ ˈnov]).
Diphthongs flatten (*pã* for `pão`, *lête* for `leite`). The signature is the **/z/ → /ʒ/**
liaison: *tás a ver* → **tájaver**. Gerúndio common, as across the south.

**Madeira** — Grouped with the centro-meridionais, but phonetically its own world and the most
caricatured accent on the mainland. **/l/ palatalises after /i/** (*famílhia*, *vilha*,
*aquilho*); **final `-e` of infinitives is kept and pronounced** (*fazêre*, *comêre*, *falare*);
a /u/ glide slips in before stressed vowels (*uôlho*, *formúiga*, *lúivro*); final `-o` closes to
`-e` (*osse*, *macaque*); `-agem` → *-age*; third-person plural preterites reduce (*ficáru*,
*fôru*). Marked preference for the **gerúndio** and for *a gente*. Vocabulary diverges too —
`semilha` for *batata* is the famous one.

**Açores** — There is no single Azorean accent; there are nine. The famous **fronted vowels** —
/u/ → **[ʏ]**, /o/ in `ou`/`oi` → **[ø]**, both genuinely unique in the Portuguese-speaking
world — are emblematic of **São Miguel**, partial in Terceira, Graciosa, eastern Pico, Flores and
Corvo, and **entirely absent** in Santa Maria, Faial, São Jorge and western Pico. A Faial speaker
sounds close to the mainland; a micaelense saying `Portugal` does not. Prosody is slower and more
sung, with longer vowels. Palatalisation after /i/ as in Madeira; nasal diphthongs simplify
(*-ôns*, *-âns*). Gerúndio reported alongside the standard. Everything a learner is taught — *tu*,
*pequeno-almoço*, *estar a + infinitivo* — is correct here; only the sound differs.

## Prompt context

The learner is in Portugal and speaks European Portuguese only. Model one neutral standard —
educated Lisbon-area pronunciation with full unstressed vowel reduction, [ʃ]/[ʒ] coda sibilants,
dark /l/, uvular /rr/, `ou` flattened, `ei` as *â-i*, and always **estar a + infinitivo**. Never
present a Brazilian form as a target: not *estou fazendo* as the learner's default, not *você* as
neutral address, not *café da manhã*, *ônibus*, *trem*, *celular* or *banheiro*, and never full
unstressed vowels or final /l/ as *w*. Separately, describe what the learner will actually hear
where they live, and mark it clearly as recognition and not as production. In the north that is
betacismo (*vinho* sounding like *binho*), preserved *ou* and *ei* diphthongs, and a whistly
apical /s/. In the Alentejo and Algarve it is slow or fast vowel collapse (*leite* as *lête*),
and the gerund *estou fazendo* — which is genuine southern European Portuguese, not a
Brazilianism, and must never be corrected in a local speaker even though the learner should not
produce it. In Madeira it is *famílhia*, *fazêre*, *semilha*; in São Miguel a French-like fronted
*u* in *tu* and *Portugal*. Say plainly that these are regional and correct, that the standard is
a register rather than a birthplace, and that copying a local accent sounds like mimicry while
using local vocabulary sounds like belonging. Where the learner lives in the Oeste or Lisbon
area, tell them they are already inside the reference variety and have no accent to adjust.
