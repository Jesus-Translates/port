# Zona: Ribatejo

## Identity

The Ribatejo is the Tejo valley — the flat, hot, agricultural middle of Portugal, upriver
from Lisbon and downhill from everywhere else. The name says it: *riba* + *Tejo*, "on the
banks of the Tejo". It is horse country and bull country, rice and vines and tomatoes, and
a string of market towns strung along the river and the railway. It is not coastal, not
mountainous, and emphatically not a resort region. People here work land, drive livestock,
mend trains, and commute.

**Administratively it does not exist, and everybody uses it anyway.** The Ribatejo was
created as a *província* by the administrative reform of **1936**, carved out of the older
Estremadura. Provinces stopped being administrative units after the 1976 constitution, so
"Ribatejo" is now a natural region and a cultural self-description rather than anything you
can put on a form. It remains completely current in speech: *sou ribatejano*, *cozinha
ribatejana*, *Feira do Ribatejo*. Sources differ on the exact number of concelhos in the
1936 province — figures of 21 and 25 both circulate — so avoid stating a count.

**What replaced it on paperwork.** Almost all of this zone is the **distrito de Santarém**,
which is what a resident actually writes down. At NUTS II level the whole zone now sits in
**Oeste e Vale do Tejo** — under the current NUTS revision (Decreto-Lei n.º 24-A/2022,
carried into the EU's NUTS 2024 version) Médio Tejo moved out of the Região Centro and
Lezíria do Tejo out of the Alentejo. So **Tomar is in the Médio Tejo, not in the Região
Centro**; never write "Tomar, na Região Centro" as current fact. Below that, in NUTS III /
intermunicipal terms the zone splits in two, and locals know the split because it decides
which body funds what:

- **Lezíria do Tejo (CIMLT)** — the downstream, flat, riverine half: Santarém, Almeirim,
  Cartaxo, Alpiarça, Chamusca, Golegã, Rio Maior, Salvaterra de Magos, Benavente, Coruche,
  Azambuja.
- **Médio Tejo (CIMT)** — the upstream, hillier half: Tomar, Abrantes, Torres Novas,
  Entroncamento, Constância, Ourém, Vila Nova da Barquinha, Alcanena, Sardoal, Mação,
  Ferreira do Zêzere.

**The three natural sub-regions** are the real mental map, and residents use these words
constantly:

- **Lezíria** — the alluvial floodplain either side of the Tejo. From Arabic *al-jazira*,
  "the island". Deep silt soils, vigorous vines, cereals, melons, tomatoes, and the pasture
  that feeds cattle and horses. This is where the campinos work.
- **Bairro** — the gently rolling country **north** of the Tejo, mixed soils, olive groves,
  vines, wheat and maize. (Note for the tutor: in this zone *bairro* has this regional
  landscape sense as well as its ordinary meaning of "neighbourhood". Both are heard.)
- **Charneca** — the sandy heath **south** of the Tejo: cork oak *montado*, eucalyptus,
  pines, and irrigated rice.

**The culture everyone recognises** is the horse and the bull. The **campino** — the mounted
herdsman of the lezíria in green cap and red waistcoat — is the region's emblem, on
municipal logos, roundabout statues and restaurant walls. Bull-running in the streets
(*largadas* and *esperas de toiros*) is an ordinary part of a summer festival programme
here in a way it is not elsewhere in Portugal. The **Lusitano** horse has its world market
at Golegã. If you are writing a lesson set in this zone and you avoid horses and bulls
entirely, you have written it about somewhere else.

**Commuting matters.** The Linha do Norte runs the length of the zone, and a great many
people work in Lisbon and live in Santarém, Cartaxo, Azambuja, Benavente or Vila Franca.
Others work in the Lisbon logistics belt around Porto Alto and Carregado. This is a region
of railway timetables and A1 traffic reports as much as of tractors.

## Geography & climate

The organising fact is the **rio Tejo**, and specifically the flat land it floods. The river
enters the zone above Abrantes, is joined by the **Zêzere** at **Constância**, runs past
Almourol, Entroncamento and Golegã, curls beneath the hill of **Santarém**, and then spreads
out into the great lezírias of Almeirim, Alpiarça, Salvaterra, Benavente and Vila Franca
before reaching the estuary.

**Rivers and water**

- **Rio Tejo** — the spine. Wide, brown, slow through the lezíria.
- **Rio Zêzere** — meets the Tejo at Constância; dammed upstream at the **Barragem de
  Castelo de Bode**, which is the zone's swimming, sailing and kayaking water.
- **Rio Nabão** — runs through the middle of Tomar; the **Mouchão** park sits on an island
  in it.
- **Rio Almonda** — rises in the karst above **Torres Novas** and runs through the town.
- **Rio Sorraia** — one of the Tejo's main tributaries, joining from the south past
  Benavente and Salvaterra de Magos.
- **Mouchões** — the islands lying between the Tejo's main channel and its banks. An
  ordinary landscape word here, not a technical one.

**Flooding is a fact of local life, not a disaster story.** The **cheias do Tejo** come in
winter, the lezíria goes under, farm tracks close, and the earth embankments that hold the
river off the fields are watched carefully. **Valada** (concelho do Cartaxo) is the classic
exposed village, protected by a long dike system — around 23 km is the figure usually
quoted *(exact length unverified)*. Residents talk about *as cheias* the way coastal people
talk about storms: routine, expected, occasionally serious.

**Reserva Natural do Estuário do Tejo** — created **19 July 1976** (Decreto-Lei n.º 565/76),
**14 192 ha**, spanning the concelhos of **Alcochete, Benavente and Vila Franca de Xira**.
It holds roughly **50 000 wintering waterbirds**, including **flamingos** (*Phoenicopterus
roseus*) and the **alfaiate** (*Recurvirostra avosetta*), of which it shelters about a
quarter of Europe's wintering population. Designated a **Ramsar** site in **1980** and a
Zona de Protecção Especial in **1994**. Its habitats are named in exactly the words locals
use: *sapais* (saltmarsh), *mouchões*, *salinas*, *paúis* and *lezírias*, almost none of it
more than 11 m above sea level.

**The western edge rises.** Around Rio Maior, Alcanena, Torres Novas and Ourém the land
climbs into limestone: the **Serra dos Candeeiros** and the **Parque Natural das Serras de
Aire e Candeeiros**, karst country full of caves. The **Almonda** karst system near Torres
Novas holds internationally important Palaeolithic sites — a *Homo heidelbergensis* skull
about **400 000 years** old came out of the **Gruta da Aroeira**.

**Climate.** Inland and continental by Portuguese standards. Summers are markedly hotter
than the coast — the lezíria bakes, and shade and the *sesta* hour are taken seriously.
Winters are cool, wet and famous for river fog (*nevoeiro*) that sits in the valley and
burns off late; the hilltop of Santarém can be in sun while the lezíria below is white.
There is no *nortada* here to speak of — that is an Oeste and Atlantic phenomenon.
Residents complain about heat in August and about fog and damp in January, and rarely about
wind. *(Numeric temperature and rainfall figures were not verified in this research pass;
keep climate language qualitative.)*

## Landmarks & buildings

- **Convento de Cristo**, Tomar — the zone's supreme monument, **UNESCO World Heritage since
  1983**. Founded as the Templar headquarters; the castle was begun in **1160** by
  **Gualdim Pais**. Its heart is the **Charola**, the round oratory modelled on the Church
  of the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem, built in two campaigns (Romanesque c. 1160–1190,
  finished Gothic c. 1230–1250). The **Janela do Capítulo** — the great Manueline window,
  begun under **Diogo de Arruda** in 1510–13 and completed by **João de Castilho** in 1515;
  crediting Arruda alone is the standard error — is the single most reproduced image in the
  region. Also the
  **Claustro de D. João III** by Diogo de Torralva, and the **Claustro da Lavagem**, built
  c. **1420** under the **Infante D. Henrique**, who was made governor of the **Ordem de
  Cristo** (founded **1357** after the Templars were suppressed) and turned its revenues
  toward the discoveries.
- **Aqueduto dos Pegões**, Tomar — designed under Filipe Terzi to feed the convent; roughly
  **6 km** long with about **180 arches** in total, the Pegões valley stretch carrying 58
  full arches on 16 pointed arches over massive piers. You can walk on it.
- **Castelo de Almourol** — the postcard castle on a granite island in the Tejo, 310 × 75 m,
  the rock standing about 18 m above the water. Templar; the inscription over the gate is
  dated *era hispânica* 1209, i.e. **1171 AD**. Nine round towers originally. It sits in the
  freguesia of **Praia do Ribatejo**, concelho de **Vila Nova da Barquinha**, and is reached
  by boat from **Tancos**, **Arripiado** or **Vila Nova da Barquinha**.
- **Portas do Sol**, Santarém — the walled garden belvedere on the edge of the hilltop,
  looking straight down over the lezíria and the Tejo. This is where Santarém takes
  visitors, where people walk in the evening, and where the view that defines the zone
  actually is.
- **Castelo de Ourém** and the **Paço dos Condes de Ourém** — the medieval hill town above
  modern Ourém, with its walled *Vila Medieval*.
- **Casa dos Patudos**, Alpiarça — the house **José Relvas** had built by the architect
  **Raul Lino** between **1905 and 1909**, left to the municipality in his **1929** will
  with the wish that it be kept as a museum. Opened as one on **15 May 1960** after a long
  inheritance dispute; classified **Imóvel de Interesse Público** in **1996**. It holds
  European and Asian painting, sculpture, ceramics and textiles, and takes roughly
  10 000–12 000 visitors a year.
- **Casa-Estúdio Carlos Relvas**, Golegã — the purpose-built photographic studio of the
  pioneer photographer, an iron-and-glass building in a garden in the middle of the town.
- **Museu Nacional Ferroviário**, Entroncamento — installed in the former CP provisions
  warehouse beside the station, reopened to the public on **19 May 2015**, covering about
  **4.5 hectares** including the **Rotunda de Locomotivas** (the roundhouse) with historic
  rolling stock.
- **Falcoaria Real de Salvaterra de Magos** — designed by **Miguel de Arruda**, one of the
  few buildings to survive the **1824** fire that destroyed the royal palace complex. The
  **Paço Real** dated from **1542**, when the Infante D. Luís received the territory, and had
  gardens, an opera house and a bullring, all lost. The town's **Praça de Touros**, opened
  in **1920**, is still in use for bullfighting through the year.
- **Salinas de Rio Maior** — working inland salt pans about 3 km outside the town, where
  brine is drawn from a well and evaporated in shallow wooden *talhos* within timber
  *marinhas*. Salt, forty kilometres from the sea.
- **Castelo de Abrantes** with the **Igreja de Santa Maria do Castelo** inside its walls,
  standing over the Tejo.
- **Castelo de Torres Novas**, and outside the town the Roman villa of **Vila Cardílio**, a
  national monument occupied in the 2nd–4th centuries.
- **CNEMA** — the **Centro Nacional de Exposições e Mercados Agrícolas** on the edge of
  Santarém, the national agricultural showground, hosting the Feira Nacional de Agricultura
  since **1994**.
- **Casa-Memória de Camões** and the **Jardim-Horto de Camões**, Constância — the poet lived
  in the town around 1546–1547.

## Churches & religious sites

- **Santuário de Fátima**, at **Cova da Iria** in the **concelho de Ourém** — by any measure
  the most visited religious site in Portugal, with around **six million visitors a year**.
  The apparitions reported by **Lúcia dos Santos**, **Francisco Marto** and **Jacinta Marto**
  took place on **13 May, 13 June, 13 July, 13 September and 13 October 1917**, with a
  further appearance on **19 August** at **Valinhos**. The **Capelinha das Aparições** was
  built between **28 April and 15 June 1919** and remains the devotional centre. The
  **Basílica de Nossa Senhora do Rosário** was begun in **1928**, consecrated **7 October
  1953**, given basilica status in **1954**, and its tower stands 65 m. The **Basílica da
  Santíssima Trindade** opened in **October 2007**. The great pilgrimages are **12–13 May**
  and **12–13 October**, when walking pilgrims (*peregrinos a pé*) fill the verges of every
  road in the zone — a genuinely everyday sight for residents, who plan journeys around it.
- **Convento de Cristo**, Tomar — a working monument rather than a parish church, but the
  town's religious identity.
- **Igreja de São João Baptista**, Tomar — the parish church on the **Praça da República**,
  and the starting point of the Santa Iria procession.
- **Santuário do Santíssimo Milagre**, Santarém, in the **Igreja de Santo Estêvão** — the
  Eucharistic miracle tradition that gave Santarém a devotional reputation of its own.
- **Igreja da Graça**, Santarém (also called Igreja de Santo Agostinho) — Gothic, and holding
  the **túmulo de Pedro Álvares Cabral**. Santarém calls itself the **Capital do Gótico**,
  and this church plus **Santa Maria de Marvila** and the **Convento de São Francisco** are
  why.
- **Convento de Santa Clara**, Santarém — founded **1259**, and the birthplace of the town's
  most famous sweet (see Food).
- **Igreja Matriz da Golegã** (Igreja de Nossa Senhora da Conceição) — 16th-century
  Manueline, on the main square where the horse fair happens around it.
- **Sinagoga de Tomar**, now the **Museu Luso-Hebraico Abraão Zacuto** — one of the very few
  surviving medieval synagogues in Portugal.
- **Nossa Senhora da Boa Viagem**, Constância — patron of the boatmen, honoured in the
  **Festa dos Barqueiros**.
- **Procissão dos Fogaréus**, Chamusca — a torch-lit Holy Week procession.

Every freguesia has its **igreja matriz** and its own **festa** with a procession; the
patronal feast, the *arraial* and the *largada de toiros* frequently share a weekend.

## Food & drink

**Protected names — use these exactly**

- `Arroz Carolino das Lezírias Ribatejanas` **IGP** — protected since **16 July 2008**, and
  the single most important product of the lezíria. A carolino rice with a low
  amylose/amylopectin ratio, very high water absorption and an unusual ability to take up
  the aromas of whatever it is cooked with; cooked, it goes soft, sticky and creamy —
  Portuguese calls the texture *natosa*. It is what makes *arroz de pato*, *arroz de
  cabidela* and *arroz doce* behave properly. Managed by **ORIVÁRZEA** (Orizicultores da
  Várzea de Samora e Benavente). **Important accuracy point:** the delimited area is
  specific freguesias of **Azambuja, Benavente, Salvaterra de Magos, Vila Franca de Xira and
  Coruche** — it does *not* cover Santarém, Almeirim or Tomar. Rice is grown widely in the
  zone; the IGP name is narrower.
- `Caralhotas de Almeirim` **IGP** — the everyday bread of Almeirim: small round loaves about
  15 cm across, brittle crackling brown crust, rustic open crumb, made from wheat flour,
  water, salt and either baker's yeast or *pão acrescentado* (dough left to leaven from the
  day before), baked 10–15 minutes in wood-fired ovens. Restricted to the concelho de
  Almeirim. **The name is genuine and is on bakery signs and IGP paperwork** — it comes from
  the local word for the little bobbles that form on wool cloth, which the scraps of dough
  left in the bottom of the mixing bowl were thought to resemble. Learners will notice the
  word looks startling; the sober etymology is the answer, and Ribatejanos enjoy watching
  outsiders hesitate before ordering.
- `Sopa da Pedra de Almeirim` — listed on the DGADR *Produtos Tradicionais Portugueses*
  register as an **ETG** (Especialidade Tradicional Garantida). *(The registration detail
  page would not load during this research pass — treat the exact registration date as
  unverified.)*

**Savoury dishes**

- `sopa da pedra` — the region's signature dish and Almeirim's claim to fame. A thick
  bean-and-pork soup: feijão encarnado, batata, couve, and a generous run of enchidos and
  pork cuts — chouriço, toucinho, orelha, and so on. The legend of the friar who asked for
  hot water and a stone and then talked his hosts into contributing everything else is told
  everywhere; the stone is real, and is still put in the bottom of the bowl. Eaten with
  caralhotas. Served in *malga de barro* to hold the heat.
- `fataça assada na telha` — river cooking at its most local. The **fataça** is the Tejo
  mullet. It is cleaned, salted, slashed and larded with strips of *toucinho*, coated with a
  sauce of onion, garlic, bay, paprika, olive oil and piri-piri, then laid in a curved red
  clay roof-tile with a second tile as a lid, the join sealed with raw bread dough so no
  sauce escapes, and roasted slowly over a wood fire or in a wood oven. Served with
  skin-on boiled potatoes and a tomato or lettuce salad. It comes from the **avieiro**
  fishing village of **Caneiras**, in the freguesia of São Nicolau, Santarém.
- `magusto` — **and this is a genuine trap.** In Santarém, *magusto* is **not** the northern
  chestnut roast. It is a savoury dish built from leftovers — couve portuguesa, boiled
  potato and stale bread dressed with olive oil and garlic, a kind of green *migas* — and it
  is served **com bacalhau assado**. Santarém holds a gastronomic festival for it. Sandra
  must not translate *magusto* as "chestnut party" for a resident of this zone.
- `açorda` — bread, garlic, coriander, olive oil and water, with egg or fish; standard
  central-southern Portuguese peasant cooking and thoroughly at home here.
- `enguias do Tejo` — river eels, eaten fried or as an *ensopado*. Strongly associated with
  the lezíria and the avieiro villages *(preparation names widely reported but not verified
  against a single canonical source)*.
- `arroz de lampreia` and river fish generally on the upper Tejo and Zêzere around
  Constância and Abrantes.
- `pão saloio ribatejano` — the region's wheat loaf, made from slightly dark flour with a
  very wet dough (over 85% water), scored horizontally with tailor's scissors before it goes
  into a dry oven so the crumb pushes out and forms a *bolha*. Sold at 400 g and 800 g.
- `melão` and `tomate` — the lezíria's two great field crops besides rice. Melon in high
  summer, tomato for processing, and everyone knows when the tomato lorries are running.

**Sweets**

- `pampilhos de Santarém` — rectangular cakes filled with **ovos-moles** and cinnamon, named
  after the campino's goad because that is the shape.
- `celestes de Santa Clara` — Santarém's conventual sweet, born in the **Convento de Santa
  Clara** (founded 1259). Almond, egg, sugar, butter and lemon, wrapped in **obreia**
  (wafer), a small cylinder about 3.5 cm high and 3 cm across, yellow on top and white at
  the sides. The recipe left the convent through a merchant, **Ajax Rato**, who sold them in
  his grocery in the centre of town; his housekeeper **Beatriz Ribeiro** worked out the
  secret and, after his death, supplied practically every pastelaria in Santarém as
  *"Beatriz dos Celestes"*.
- `arrepiados de Santarém` — the other Santarém sweet named alongside the celestes as a
  district finalist for the *7 Maravilhas Doces de Portugal*.
- `queijadinhas de Santarém`.
- `fatias de Tomar`, `queijadas de Tomar`, and `beija-me depressa` — Tomar's trio.
- `palha de Abrantes` — the fine egg-thread sweet that Abrantes is known for.
- `arroz doce` — made properly here, with the local carolino, and on every family table.

**Drink**

- **The wine region changed its name in 2009: `Ribatejo` became `Tejo`.** Modern bottles say
  **Vinho Regional Tejo** (IGP) or **DOC do Tejo**. Around **12 000 hectares** produce some
  **65 million litres** a year, roughly a tenth of Portugal's output, of which about 90% is
  IGP and 10% DOC. The region runs from Tomar, Ferreira do Zêzere, Sardoal and Mação in the
  north, through Abrantes, Chamusca, Alpiarça and Almeirim, west to Torres Novas, Alcanena,
  Rio Maior, Cartaxo and Azambuja, and south to Coruche and Benavente. The six old
  sub-regional names — **Almeirim, Cartaxo, Chamusca, Coruche, Santarém, Tomar** — were
  folded into the single designation in 2009, though they still turn up informally on labels
  and constantly in conversation. **Note:** sources disagree on whether the DOC formally
  retained "Ribatejo"; the names actually printed on current bottles are *Tejo* and *DOC do
  Tejo*, so use those.
- **Cartaxo** calls itself the **Capital do Vinho** and is the Lezíria do Tejo's biggest red
  producer.
- `vinho medieval de Ourém` — holds VQPRD status since **11 February 2005**, with hundreds
  of registered growers in the concelho.
- `água-pé` — the light, low-alcohol wine drunk with roast chestnuts at São Martinho, and
  therefore the drink of the Golegã fair.

## Restaurants & institutions

Genuinely long-standing houses, verified as trading during this research pass. Where a place
could not be confirmed, it is not listed.

- **Taberna do Quinzena**, Rua Pedro de Santarém 93-95, Santarém — trading since **1870**,
  four generations of the same family, currently run by **Fernando Batista** (whose father
  was also Fernando — do not attach the father's doings to the son). The name comes from
  the field workers who settled their slate *à quinzena*, fortnightly. Walls solid with
  *tauromaquia* — **cartazes**, **capotes**, **farpas** and **barretes de campino** — a
  family archive rather than a theme. The ritual is the sharing: you sit down the table
  from people you did not arrive with, and the *porco preto* and the *bacalhau frito com
  cebolada* come to be split. The family now runs several houses in Santarém, including
  **Taberna do Quinzena Mercado** (opened 2006) and **Taberna do Quinzena II** at Mecheira.
  *(Do not say "Quinzena Hotel" — no such thing. They run the restaurant side of the
  **Hotel Verdi Santarém**, which is somebody else's hotel.)*
- **O Toucinho**, Almeirim — opened in **1962** as a *taberna e mercearia*, the first eating
  house in Almeirim, and the place where **sopa da pedra** first went on a menu. The founder
  was **José Manuel Toucinho**; two travelling suppliers passing through asked the family to
  serve them some of the soup, came back weeks later unable to name it, and described it by
  the colour of the pavement — and the rest of the town's economy followed. It is run today
  by his daughter and son-in-law, **Hélia and João Simões**, with their sons in the kitchen;
  **do not credit João Simões with the 1962 story**. It still puts the stone in the bottom
  of the bowl and still bakes its own **caralhotas** in its own wood oven. It is **closed on
  Thursdays**. The ordering ritual is fixed: the soup arrives, you fish for the stone,
  someone at the table makes the joke about it being the same stone since 1962, and everyone
  agrees that their own family's version is better while eating a second bowl.

**The argument you will hear.** Almeirim has a whole avenue of houses serving sopa da pedra
and the town takes the comparison seriously. Asking a Ribatejano which one is best is a
reliable way to lose twenty minutes, and the correct move for a learner is to say *"cada um
tem a sua"* and let them tell you anyway. Never take a side, and never repeat criticism of
a named house — it will get back.

**Institutions residents actually name**

- **CNEMA**, Santarém — Centro Nacional de Exposições e Mercados Agrícolas. The national
  agricultural showground, hosting the Feira Nacional de Agricultura since 1994. In June the
  whole city reorganises around it.
- **Companhia das Lezírias**, headquartered at **Samora Correia** — founded **1836**,
  nationalised **13 November 1975** and today a wholly state-owned company. The largest
  agro-forestry operation in Portugal: cork, rice, wine, olive oil, cattle and horses across
  the Lezíria of Vila Franca, the Charneca do Infantado, Catapereiro and the *paúis* of
  Magos, Belmonte and Lavouras. Since **2013** it also manages the national studs
  (*coudelarias*). For a lot of families in Benavente and Salvaterra it is simply "a
  Companhia" — an employer, a landlord and a landmark at once.
- **ORIVÁRZEA** — the rice growers' organisation behind the carolino IGP.
- **Museu Nacional Ferroviário**, Entroncamento — the town's civic identity in a building.
- **Museu Rural e do Vinho do Cartaxo**, with guided visits, tastings and sales, and the
  **Rota do Vinho** built around it.
- **Casa dos Patudos**, Alpiarça — a museum a town of under 7 000 people is disproportionately
  proud of.
- **Complexo Desportivo de Rio Maior** — the reason Rio Maior calls itself the *cidade do
  Desporto*: an Olympic pool and a high-performance training centre that placed third
  internationally in the 2007 IOC/IAKS Award.
- **Grupo de Forcados Amadores da Chamusca** — the kind of association that structures social
  life in a small Ribatejo town: the *tertúlia*, the dinners, the season.
- **Escola Superior Agrária de Santarém** *(Instituto Politécnico de Santarém — presence
  well known locally but unverified in this research pass)*.

## Everyday life & errands

**The railway is the zone's nervous system.** The **Linha do Norte** runs the length of the
Ribatejo, and residents plan their lives on it.

- **Entroncamento** is the junction that gives the town its name — *entroncamento* is simply
  the common noun for a railway junction. Here the Linha do Norte meets the lines heading
  east: the **Linha do Leste** towards Elvas and Badajoz, and the **Linha da Beira Baixa**
  towards Castelo Branco, Covilhã and Guarda. Almost every long-distance train in the
  country passes through, and for generations most of the town worked for the railway.
- **Santarém's station is not in Santarém.** It sits down in the valley at **Santa Iria da
  Ribeira de Santarém**, at 22 m altitude (PK 74+400), while the city is up on its hill.
  Getting from platform to town means the **Scalabus** (lines 2, 3 and 4) or a taxi. Every
  visitor is caught out by this once, and every resident has a view on the connection times.
  **Alfa Pendular**, **Intercidades**, **InterRegional** and **Regional** services all call
  there; trains to Lisbon run to **Santa Apolónia** via **Oriente**.
- **The Lisbon urban network stops at Azambuja.** The *Comboios Urbanos de Lisboa* line runs
  Lisboa–Azambuja, so a commuter from Azambuja is on urban fares and frequencies, while a
  commuter from Santarém one stop further up is on Regional or Intercidades tickets. That
  single boundary shapes where people choose to live.
- **Tomar is on a branch, not the main line.** The **Ramal de Tomar** leaves the Linha do
  Norte at **Lamarosa**, not at Entroncamento as people often assume. It is **14.8 km**,
  single track, electrified at 25 kV, opened **24 September 1928**, with stops at Soudos-Vila
  Nova, Carrascal-Delongo, Curvaceiras, Santa Cita and Carvalhos de Figueiredo before Tomar.
- **Torres Novas and Golegã are served from Riachos**, a few kilometres out of town — another
  station-is-elsewhere situation *(exact current station naming unverified)*.
- Road-wise: the **A1** and the **A23** carry the traffic, the **Ponte da Lezíria** between
  Carregado and Benavente and the **Ponte Marechal Carmona** at Vila Franca de Xira are the
  two big Tejo crossings downstream, and locals will tell you which is quicker today.

**Markets and shopping**

- **Mercado Municipal de Santarém** — **Tuesday to Friday, 07:00 to 15:00, and Saturday
  07:00 to 14:00**, closed Sunday and Monday. It **reopened on 19 March 2025** after nearly six years
  shut for rebuilding, which was a genuine civic event; people had been complaining about
  the closure for years and now compare the new stalls with the old ones.
- Santarém also runs a **Mercado Quinzenal**, a fortnightly market on the edge of town
  *(exact days unverified — do not name a day)*.
- **Ourém** holds its municipal market — the **Mercado Municipal Manuel Prazeres Durão** —
  on **Thursday and Saturday mornings**.
- Most other concelhos run a monthly or fortnightly *feira* on a fixed day. **Do not invent
  one.** If a lesson needs a market day, use Santarém's Tuesday-to-Saturday indoor market or
  Ourém's Thursday and Saturday mornings, both of which are documented.

**Bureaucracy.** **Santarém is the capital de distrito**, so district-level offices sit
there and a resident of Golegã or Chamusca expects to drive in for anything above municipal
level. Below that, the ordinary sequence is the same as everywhere: **Junta de Freguesia**
for *atestados* and residence certificates, **Câmara Municipal** for licences and rates,
**Espaço Cidadão** / **Loja do Cidadão** for the *cartão de cidadão*, **Finanças** for the
NIF and IMI, **Centro de Saúde** for the *número de utente*, **Conservatória** for registry
matters. The zone's two intermunicipal bodies, **CIMLT** and **CIMT**, run cross-municipal
services and appear on signage and funding plaques constantly.

**Agricultural rhythms** structure the year visibly, and any lesson set here can hang on
them:

- **Winter** — the *cheias*; the lezíria under water; pruning the vines; fog.
- **Spring** — rice fields flooded and sown; the lezíria turns green; the horses out.
- **Summer** — melons and tomatoes; tomato lorries on every road; cork stripping
  (*descortiçamento*) in the charneca; *largadas de toiros* at every festa; the heat.
- **Late summer / autumn** — the *vindima*; the rice harvest; the *ferra* and *apartação*
  of cattle; São Martinho and Golegã in November.

**The river as daily fact.** People fish it, cross it, watch it, and complain about it. The
**avieiro** villages — **Escaroupim** (Salvaterra de Magos), **Caneiras** (Santarém),
**Palhota**, **Patacão**, **Valada do Ribatejo** — are the descendants of fishing families
who came down from **Vieira de Leiria** in the 19th century, first wintering in their boats,
then building wooden **palafitas** on stilts above the flood line. The **Museu "Escaroupim e
o Rio"** keeps their gear and photographs. Their houses on legs, painted in strong colours,
are one of the most recognisable sights in the lezíria.

**Bull culture as a scheduled event.** An **espera de toiros** is the crowd waiting in the
street as the herd is driven through by mounted campinos and their *cabrestos* towards the
ring or an enclosure; a **largada de toiros** is a single bull released into a
barrier-fenced stretch of street, then collected by the campinos with the halter oxen. These
are printed in municipal festival programmes with times, like any other event, and families
plan around them.

## Festivals & calendar

- **Festa dos Tabuleiros**, Tomar — **every four years**, in early July. The most recent was
  **2023**; the next is **2027**. Get this cycle right: it is not annual, and a learner in
  Tomar will know the year immediately. A **tabuleiro** is a wicker basket wrapped in
  embroidered white cloth, with **30 loaves of 400 g** threaded onto canes, dressed with
  paper flowers, wheat and greenery, and crowned with either the **Pomba do Espírito Santo**
  or the **Cruz de Cristo**; it is carried on the head by a young woman in white and is
  built to her own height. The **cortejo** covers about **5 km** through streets carpeted
  with paper flowers, and drew over **600 000** people in 2019. It descends from the *festas
  do Espírito Santo* associated with **D. Dinis** and the **Rainha Santa Isabel**. The day
  after the procession comes the **Pêza**, the sharing out of bread and meat among the
  people (the general Portuguese term for this is the *Bodo*).
- **Feira Nacional de Agricultura / Feira do Ribatejo**, CNEMA, Santarém — nine days in
  **June**. In 2025 it ran **7–15 June** as the **61.ª FNA / 71.ª Feira do Ribatejo**; in
  2026, **6–14 June**. The Feira do Ribatejo dates from **1954**, the national fair from
  **1964**, and CNEMA has hosted them since **1994**. Livestock competitions of *raças
  autóctones* — cattle, horses, hens, goats, sheep, pigs — machinery, debates, crafts,
  gastronomy and a concert programme. **Accuracy note:** "**Festa Nacional do Gado**" is a
  name that circulates for the livestock side of this fair, but this research pass could not
  confirm it as a separate current event. Use the official names *Feira Nacional de
  Agricultura* and *Feira do Ribatejo*.
- **Feira Nacional do Cavalo**, Golegã — **November**, ten days built around **São Martinho
  on 11 November**. The 2025 edition ran **7–16 November** as the **XLIX Feira Nacional do
  Cavalo**, alongside the **XXVI Feira Internacional do Cavalo Lusitano** and the historic
  **Feira de São Martinho**, whose origin is traced to **1571**; the modern name dates from
  **1972**. Golegã is the world's main trading floor for the **Puro Sangue Lusitano**.
  Riders fill the streets, there are jumping and driving competitions and displays by the
  **Escola Nacional de Equitação**, and everyone drinks **água-pé** with roast **castanhas**
  because it is São Martinho. *(A widely repeated line linking the fair's competitive era to
  the Marquês de Pombal does not survive a date check — Pombal died in 1782 — so do not
  repeat it.)*
- **Colete Encarnado**, Vila Franca de Xira — **always the first weekend of July**, first
  held in **1932** on the initiative of **José Van-Zeller Pereira Palha**. Three days of
  **largadas de toiros** through the streets, a **garraiada** early on the Sunday, touradas,
  a **Missa Rociera**, boat gatherings on the river, processions of campinos and carriages,
  the presentation of the **Pampilho de Honra** to a chosen campino, and fireworks over the
  marina at midnight on the Monday. **Note:** Vila Franca de Xira is in the **distrito de
  Lisboa** and the Área Metropolitana de Lisboa, not in this zone's municipality list — but
  it is historically Ribatejo and this is the festival everyone in the zone knows.
- **Feira de Santa Iria**, Tomar — about **ten days in October**, running from the Mercado
  Municipal along the Rua dos Arcos to the **Várzea Grande**: funfair, food stalls, local
  produce, crafts, a **Feira dos Frutos Secos** near the courthouse, and concerts. The feast
  of the padroeira **Santa Iria** falls on **20 October**, with a procession from the Igreja
  de São João Baptista that ends with petals thrown into the **Nabão** from the **Ponte
  Velha** *(exact dates shift year to year — check before naming them)*.
- **Peregrinações a Fátima** — **12–13 May** and **12–13 October**, the two great pilgrimages.
  For residents of Ourém and everywhere along the walking routes, these are traffic events
  as much as religious ones, and hospitality is a local reflex: water, chairs, a place to
  sit.
- **Festa do Vinho**, Cartaxo — **1 May**.
- **Feira dos Santos**, Cartaxo — **1 November**.
- **ALPIAGRA**, Alpiarça — the municipality's agricultural and commercial fair, running since
  **1983**.
- **Feira da Cebola**, Rio Maior — the onion fair, whose origin the municipality traces to
  **1761**.
- **Feira da Ascensão** and the **Procissão dos Fogaréus**, Chamusca.
- **Festa da Amizade**, Benavente — grilled sardines and bulls.
- **Festa dos Barqueiros / Nossa Senhora da Boa Viagem**, Constância — the boatmen's feast,
  kept around Easter.
- **Festival do Arroz Carolino das Lezírias Ribatejanas** — the rice festival in the Benavente
  and Salvaterra lezíria.
- **Festival Gastronómico do Magusto**, Santarém — restaurants across the city cooking the
  bacalhau-and-bread *magusto*, traditional and modern versions.
- Through **June to September**, expect *arraiais*, *largadas* and *esperas* in almost every
  town on its patronal weekend.

## Language notes

**Where the accent sits.** The Ribatejo is squarely in the **centro-meridional**
(central-southern) group of European Portuguese dialects. The features that group is defined
by are documented and safe to teach:

- The **/b/–/v/ distinction is kept** (unlike the north, where *vaca* can sound like *baca*).
- **The diphthongs `ei` and `ou` are monophthongised**: `ei` → [e̞] and `ou` → [o̞]. So
  *primeiro* comes out close to **primêro**, *dinheiro* as **dinhêro**, *pouco* as **pôco**,
  *outro* as **ôtro**. This is the single most audible thing about how the zone speaks, and
  it is shared with Lisbon and the south rather than being unique to the Ribatejo.
- Southern speech generally is described as **more drawn-out** (*mais arrastado*) than
  Lisbon's, with `ãe` tending towards **ên**. The eastern Ribatejo shades into features of
  **Alentejan** Portuguese.

**Honest limitation:** the "*inconfundível sotaque ribatejano*" is constantly referred to,
but this research pass found **no rigorous published phonetic description** specific to the
Ribatejo as distinct from the wider centro-meridional group. Sandra should therefore teach
the group features above with confidence, describe the rhythm as slower and more open than
Lisbon's, and **not invent** signature Ribatejo pronunciations.

**Register.** Standard European Portuguese, *tu* with family and neighbours, *o senhor / a
senhora* at the counter, *pequeno-almoço*, *estar a fazer*. Rural courtesy is warm and
direct; *ó* before a name or a term of address (*ó pá*, *ó menina*) is very common in
informal speech across this whole region *(widely observed, not verified against a
linguistic source)*.

**Vocabulary a resident uses**

Landscape and the river:
- `lezíria` — the Tejo floodplain; from Arabic *al-jazira*, "the island"
- `mouchão` — a river island between the main channel and the bank
- `charneca` — sandy heath and cork-oak country south of the Tejo
- `bairro` — here also the rolling farmland north of the Tejo, not only "neighbourhood"
- `montado` — the managed cork-oak and holm-oak landscape
- `cheias` — the winter floods of the Tejo
- `mota` — the earth dike holding the river off the fields *(term unverified in this pass)*
- `sapal` — saltmarsh
- `paul` (pl. `paúis`) — marsh, fen
- `marinha` — a salt pan; `talho` — the shallow wooden evaporation bed inside it
- `vala` — a drainage channel across the lezíria

Horses, bulls and the campino:
- `campino` — the mounted herdsman of the lezíria
- `pampilho` — his goad: a straight young eucalyptus shoot, about 250–270 cm, with a metal
  ferrule and a removable spike at the tip
- `barrete` — the green cap with red trim and a wool tassel
- `colete encarnado` — the red waistcoat; `faixa`/`cinta` — the red wool sash
- `cabrestos` — the halter oxen, usually seven, mostly **Mertolenga**, each with a brass bell
  and metal balls on the horn tips, that steer the herd
- `manada` — a herd under one man's charge; `maioral` — the senior herdsman over it
- `ganadaria` — the estate that breeds fighting bulls
- `apartação` — separating and driving bulls to the loading pens
- `ferra` — the branding of young stock; `tenta` — testing heifers for breeding
- `transumância` — the seasonal move between pastures
- `espera de toiros` — the crowd waiting as the herd is driven through the streets
- `largada de toiros` — one bull loosed into a barriered stretch of street
- `garraiada` — a bullfight with young animals, often at dawn during a festa
- `forcados` — the group that faces the bull on foot
- `tertúlia` — the bullfighting club and its endless talk
- `Puro Sangue Lusitano` — the Lusitano horse breed
- `coudelaria` — a stud farm

Food and drink:
- `caralhotas` — Almeirim's IGP bread; also the local word for bobbles on wool cloth
- `pão acrescentado` — leftover leavened dough used as the next day's starter
- `arroz carolino` — the lezíria's rice, the one that goes creamy
- `natosa` — the creamy, velvety texture cooked carolino gives
- `fataça` — the Tejo mullet
- `telha` — the curved clay roof-tile it is roasted in
- `malga` — the earthenware bowl sopa da pedra arrives in
- `obreia` — the wafer wrapping a celeste
- `ovos-moles` — the egg-and-sugar cream filling a pampilho
- `enchidos` — cured sausages; `toucinho` — cured pork fat
- `água-pé` — the light wine of São Martinho
- `vindima` — the grape harvest; `descortiçamento` — the stripping of cork
- `magusto` — **here, the bacalhau-bread-and-cabbage dish, not a chestnut roast**

River people and railways:
- `avieiro` — a member of the Tejo fishing communities descended from Vieira de Leiria
- `palafita` — a house built on stilts above the flood line
- `entroncamento` — a railway junction (the common noun that became a town's name)
- `ramal` — a branch line; `linha` — a main line
- `apeadeiro` — an unstaffed halt, as opposed to an `estação`

## Towns

### Santarém

**Known for:** being the capital of the district and the self-declared **Capital do Gótico**;
the Feira Nacional de Agricultura; the view. A city of about 30 000 in a municipality of
around 58 700 across 18 freguesias (2021).

**Landmark:** the **Portas do Sol**, a walled garden on the lip of the hill looking straight
down the Tejo and across the lezíria — the defining view of the whole zone. Behind it, the
Gothic set: **Igreja da Graça** with the tomb of **Pedro Álvares Cabral**, **Santa Maria de
Marvila**, the **Convento de São Francisco**, and the **Santuário do Santíssimo Milagre** in
the Igreja de Santo Estêvão.

**Food:** **pampilhos** and **celestes de Santa Clara**; the savoury **magusto com bacalhau**
with its own gastronomic festival; **fataça na telha** from the avieiro village of
**Caneiras** just downstream.

**Everyday life:** the **Mercado Municipal**, Tuesday to Friday 07:00–15:00 and Saturday
07:00–14:00, reopened in March 2025 after nearly six years of works. The railway station is
down in the valley at **Santa Iria da Ribeira de Santarém**, reached from town by
**Scalabus** 2, 3 or 4. In June the entire city bends around **CNEMA**.

### Tomar

**Known for:** the Templars, the **Convento de Cristo**, and the **Festa dos Tabuleiros**
every four years (2023, next 2027). On the **rio Nabão**, in the Médio Tejo.

**Landmark:** the **Convento de Cristo**, UNESCO-listed since **1983** — the **Charola**, the
**Janela do Capítulo**, the **Claustro de D. João III** — with the **Castelo** founded in
1160 by Gualdim Pais, the **Mata Nacional dos Sete Montes** behind it, and the **Aqueduto
dos Pegões** striding across the valley outside town.

**Food:** **fatias de Tomar**, **queijadas** and **beija-me depressa**. Every pastelaria has
an opinion about the right pan for fatias.

**Everyday life:** the **Praça da República** with the **Igreja de São João Baptista** is the
town's living room. The **Feira de Santa Iria** takes over ten days of October, running from
the Mercado Municipal down Rua dos Arcos to the Várzea Grande. Trains are a branch-line
affair: the **Ramal de Tomar** joins the main line at **Lamarosa**, and the change is part
of every journey to Lisbon.

### Abrantes

**Known for:** commanding the upper Tejo from a hilltop; a commercial and industrial hub —
metalworking and machinery — for a large farming hinterland. Around 36 100 people across
714.69 km² and 13 freguesias, the largest concelho in the zone by area.

**Landmark:** the **Castelo de Abrantes** with the **Igreja de Santa Maria do Castelo**
inside the walls, looking out over the river; two bridges, road and rail, to the south bank.

**Food:** **palha de Abrantes**, the fine egg-thread sweet the town is named for in every
Portuguese confectionery book.

**Everyday life:** the **Barragem de Castelo de Bode** upstream on the Zêzere is the local
water — sailing, kayaking, swimming — and where people go in August. Municipal holiday is
**14 June**; the patrons are São Vicente and São João.

### Entroncamento

**Known for:** being a town that the railway built from nothing. It became a freguesia in
**1926**, a concelho in **1945** and a city on **20 June 1991**. **20 141** people packed
into **13.73 km²** at nearly 1 467 per km².

**Landmark:** the **Museu Nacional Ferroviário**, in the old CP provisions warehouse by the
station — 4.5 hectares including the **Rotunda de Locomotivas**. The municipal coat of arms
has rails and signals on it.

**Food:** no distinct town dish; it eats the regional repertoire, and its restaurants have
always run on railway shift patterns.

**Everyday life:** this is the junction where the **Linha do Norte** meets the lines east —
the **Linha do Leste** to Elvas and Badajoz, the **Linha da Beira Baixa** to Castelo Branco
and Covilhã. **Correct a common myth:** Entroncamento is often called the smallest
municipality in Portugal. It is the **second** least extensive in the mainland, not the
first.

### Golegã

**Known for:** horses, and very little else, which is exactly how the town likes it. About
**5 442** people across three freguesias — Golegã, Azinhaga and Pombalinho — and for ten days
in November the world's Lusitano market.

**Landmark:** the **Igreja Matriz** (Nossa Senhora da Conceição), 16th-century Manueline, on
the square the fair happens around; the **Casa-Estúdio Carlos Relvas**, the photographer's
iron-and-glass studio in its garden; the **Quinta da Cardiga**.

**Food:** the town has a real restaurant culture for its size. In November it runs on
**água-pé** and roast **castanhas**, because the fair is built on **São Martinho**, 11
November.

**Everyday life:** eleven months of a quiet farming town, and then the **Feira Nacional do
Cavalo** — 7–16 November in 2025 — when riders outnumber cars in the streets and every yard
and gateway is full of horses. The nearest station is **Riachos**, shared with Torres Novas.

### Almeirim

**Known for:** **sopa da pedra**, to a degree that has reshaped the town's economy. About
**22 016** people across four freguesias: Almeirim, **Fazendas de Almeirim**, **Benfica do
Ribatejo** and **Raposa**.

**Landmark:** the site of the **Paço Real**, built 1411–1423 under **D. João I**, which made
Almeirim a royal winter retreat known as the *Sintra de Inverno*; the **Cortes de Almeirim
de 1580** met here to argue the succession after D. Sebastião's death.

**Food:** **sopa da pedra** with the stone in the bottom of the bowl, eaten with
**caralhotas de Almeirim** IGP; the melons, pork and wines of the lezíria.

**Everyday life:** the restaurant strip is a genuine local industry, and locals argue about
the houses with the seriousness other towns reserve for football. **O Toucinho**, open since
**1962**, is where the dish first went on a menu.

### Cartaxo

**Known for:** wine. It calls itself the **Capital do Vinho**, is the Lezíria do Tejo's
second-largest producer overall and its largest producer of red. About **23 187** people
across six freguesias — Pontével, Vale da Pinta, Vila Chã de Ourique, Valada, Ereira, Lapa.

**Landmark:** the **Museu Rural e do Vinho do Cartaxo**, with guided visits, tastings and
sales, and the **Rota do Vinho** built around it.

**Food and drink:** Tejo reds above all; the **Festa do Vinho** on **1 May** is the year's
fixed point, and the **Feira dos Santos** on **1 November** the other.

**Everyday life:** the Tejo runs about 12 km through the concelho. **Valada** is the exposed
riverside village, protected by its long dike, and one of the **avieiro** settlements. The
town is close enough to Lisbon that a large share of residents commute.

### Torres Novas

**Known for:** the castle in the middle of town, the **rio Almonda**, and some of the most
important Palaeolithic archaeology in Europe. **34 149** people across ten freguesias in
about 270 km².

**Landmark:** the **Castelo de Torres Novas**, whose walls enclose a garden used as an
ordinary public park; the Roman villa of **Vila Cardílio**; the **Grutas das Lapas**; and in
the Almonda karst the **Gruta da Aroeira**, which produced a *Homo heidelbergensis* skull
around 400 000 years old.

**Food:** the regional repertoire, with the **Museu Municipal Carlos Reis** and a set of
ethnographic and agricultural museums out in the freguesias documenting how it was farmed.

**Everyday life:** pottery is still made at **Argea**; the **Clube Desportivo de Torres
Novas**, founded 1925, anchors sporting life. Trains are caught at **Riachos**, outside the
town.

### Ourém

**Known for:** containing **Fátima**. The concelho has **two cities** — Ourém and Fátima —
13 freguesias, 416.68 km², and **44 576** inhabitants, of whom only about 6 000 live in
Ourém city itself. The gap between the quiet county town and the six-million-visitor
sanctuary eight kilometres away is the defining local fact.

**Landmark:** the **Castelo de Ourém** and the **Paço dos Condes de Ourém** crowning the
medieval hill town; and, at **Cova da Iria**, the **Capelinha das Aparições**, the
**Basílica de Nossa Senhora do Rosário** (consecrated 7 October 1953) and the **Basílica da
Santíssima Trindade** (opened October 2007).

**Food:** `vinho medieval de Ourém`, holding VQPRD status since **11 February 2005**, with
hundreds of registered growers in the concelho.

**Everyday life:** the municipal market runs **Thursday and Saturday mornings**. Around **12–13 May**
and **12–13 October** the roads fill with walking pilgrims, and residents plan journeys,
deliveries and school runs accordingly — offering water and a chair to *peregrinos* is an
ordinary neighbourly act here.

**Also in this zone**, and fully usable in lessons: **Rio Maior** (the inland **salinas** with
their wooden *marinhas* and *talhos*, the **Feira da Cebola**, and the *cidade do Desporto*
sports complex); **Salvaterra de Magos** (the **Falcoaria Real** by Miguel de Arruda, the
1920 **Praça de Touros**, the **avieiro** village of **Escaroupim**, and rice across the
lezíria); **Benavente** (rice, the **Reserva Natural do Estuário do Tejo**, the **rio
Sorraia**, and **Samora Correia**, home of the **Companhia das Lezírias**); **Alpiarça** (one
of only six Portuguese municipalities with a single freguesia; the **Casa dos Patudos** and
the **ALPIAGRA** fair); **Chamusca** (montado, cork, rice, and a tauromachy tradition strong
enough that the town is called the heart of the Ribatejo — the **Grupo de Forcados Amadores
da Chamusca**, the **Feira da Ascensão**, the **Procissão dos Fogaréus**); and **Constância**
(where the **Zêzere** meets the **Tejo**, renamed from *Punhete* by D. Maria II in **1836**,
with the **Casa-Memória de Camões** and the **Festa dos Barqueiros**).

## Prompt context

The learner lives in the Ribatejo, the Tejo valley inland from Lisbon: Santarém on its hill
above the lezíria, Tomar on the Nabão, the railway town of Entroncamento, Golegã's horses,
Almeirim's sopa da pedra. Ground lessons in flat alluvial farmland — arroz carolino, melons,
tomatoes, vines — and in the horse-and-bull culture everyone here grew up around: the campino
in his barrete verde and colete encarnado, the pampilho, the largadas and esperas de toiros
that close a town's streets in summer. Daily life runs on the Linha do Norte (Santarém's
station sits down by the river at Santa Iria da Ribeira, a Scalabus ride below the town), on
the Mercado Municipal, on the câmara and the junta de freguesia, and on the river's moods —
the cheias that flood the lezíria each winter. Use the Tejo, the mouchões, the avieiro
fishing villages on their palafitas, and the wine now labelled Tejo rather than Ribatejo.
Sweets are pampilhos, celestes, fatias de Tomar. Speech is central-southern European
Portuguese: primeiro sounds like primêro, pouco like pôco, slower and more drawn out than
Lisbon. European Portuguese only, tu register.
